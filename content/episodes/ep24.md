## Overview

A four-bar pop chorus can loop inside your head all day. Not Beethoven, not Coltrane — the mathematically *least surprising* thing in music. In {{< episode-ref ep="7" >}}EP07{{< /episode-ref >}} we defined entropy as average surprise; a chorus has low entropy. So why can't you shake it?

The answer involves three interlocking information-theoretic clues. First, the chorus is *compressible*: your brain can re-describe it as a handful of splice-able fragments, so a single fragment auto-completes the rest. Second, compressibility alone is not enough — a single repeating tone compresses to almost nothing, yet nobody wants to listen. There is an optimal *complexity sweet spot* around 30–40% LZ compression ratio where listener preference peaks. Third, pop songs are not uniformly predictable; they deploy *precise violations* — sharply elevated surprisal values at strategic moments (the chorus entry, a key change, the climactic note) — while remaining flat elsewhere.

This episode builds the formal machinery: Shannon entropy and self-information, Kolmogorov complexity and its relationship to LZ77 compression, mutual information between melody and harmony, and the surprisal-profile comparison between human-composed pop and early AI-generated melodies.

## Definitions

{{< definition name="Self-Information (Surprisal)" label="Def 24.1" >}}
Let {{< m >}}X{{< /m >}} be a discrete random variable over alphabet {{< m >}}\mathcal{A}{{< /m >}} with probability mass function {{< m >}}p{{< /m >}}. The **self-information** (or **surprisal**) of outcome {{< m >}}x \in \mathcal{A}{{< /m >}} is

{{< dm >}}I(x) = -\log_2 p(x) \quad \text{(bits).}{{< /dm >}}

Self-information is a decreasing function of probability: certain events ({{< m >}}p = 1{{< /m >}}) carry zero surprise; impossible events carry infinite surprise. Shannon entropy is the expectation of self-information:

{{< dm >}}H(X) = \mathbb{E}[I(X)] = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{A}} p(x) \log_2 p(x).{{< /dm >}}
{{< /definition >}}

**Worked example.** Model the next pitch class in a C-major melody as a random variable with an empirical tonal distribution. Suppose the preceding context is C–D–E–F. The transition probability to G (the scale degree V) is approximately {{< m >}}p(G \mid \text{C-D-E-F}) \approx 0.60{{< /m >}}, while the probability of B{{< m >}}\flat{{< /m >}} (a chromatic outsider) is approximately {{< m >}}p(B\flat) \approx 0.05{{< /m >}}. Then:

{{< dm >}}I(G) = -\log_2(0.60) \approx 0.74 \text{ bits}, \qquad I(B\flat) = -\log_2(0.05) \approx 4.32 \text{ bits.}{{< /dm >}}

The B{{< m >}}\flat{{< /m >}} carries nearly six times as much surprise. This is the information-theoretic skeleton of the "jarring note" sensation.

{{< definition name="Conditional Surprisal and Conditional Entropy" label="Def 24.2" >}}
Given a context (conditioning event) {{< m >}}c{{< /m >}}, the **conditional surprisal** of outcome {{< m >}}x{{< /m >}} is

{{< dm >}}I(x \mid c) = -\log_2 p(x \mid c).{{< /dm >}}

The **conditional entropy** of {{< m >}}X{{< /m >}} given {{< m >}}C{{< /m >}} is

{{< dm >}}H(X \mid C) = \mathbb{E}_{c}[-\log_2 p(X \mid C = c)] = -\sum_{c} p(c) \sum_{x} p(x \mid c) \log_2 p(x \mid c).{{< /dm >}}

For melody analysis, we set {{< m >}}C{{< /m >}} = the preceding {{< m >}}k{{< /m >}} notes (a {{< m >}}k{{< /m >}}-gram context). A lower {{< m >}}H(X \mid C){{< /m >}} means the melody is more predictable given its recent history.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="LZ77 Compression and Compression Ratio" label="Def 24.3" >}}
The **LZ77 algorithm** (Ziv & Lempel 1977) compresses a string {{< m >}}s = s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n{{< /m >}} using a *sliding window* of width {{< m >}}W{{< /m >}}. At each position {{< m >}}i{{< /m >}}, the encoder searches the window {{< m >}}s_{i-W} \cdots s_{i-1}{{< /m >}} for the longest match to the upcoming substring starting at {{< m >}}s_i{{< /m >}}. A match of length {{< m >}}\ell{{< /m >}} at offset {{< m >}}d{{< /m >}} is encoded as a triple {{< m >}}(d, \ell, s_{i+\ell}){{< /m >}}: a *back-reference pointer* plus the first mismatching character.

The **LZ compression ratio** is

{{< dm >}}\rho(s) = \frac{|LZ77(s)|}{|s|}{{< /dm >}}

where {{< m >}}|{}\cdot{}|{{< /m >}} denotes bit-length. Strings with many repeated patterns have {{< m >}}\rho \ll 1{{< /m >}}; incompressible strings have {{< m >}}\rho \approx 1{{< /m >}}.
{{< /definition >}}

**Worked example.** Encode the pitch-class sequence of a pop hook as a string over the alphabet {{< m >}}\{0,1,\ldots,11\}{{< /m >}}. A four-bar chorus with the pattern C–E–G–E / C–E–G–E (notes 0–4–7–4 repeated) has back-references of length 4 pointing just 4 steps back; the compressed output is roughly 30% of the raw length. A jazz improvisation over the same duration with non-repeating chromatic runs compresses to about 55% of raw length. A uniformly random pitch sequence compresses to about 95%.

{{< definition name="Kolmogorov Complexity" label="Def 24.4" >}}
Fix a universal Turing machine {{< m >}}\mathcal{U}{{< /m >}}. The **Kolmogorov complexity** of a finite string {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}} is

{{< dm >}}K(s) = \min\{|\pi| : \mathcal{U}(\pi) = s\}{{< /dm >}}

where the minimum is over all programs {{< m >}}\pi{{< /m >}} (binary strings) such that {{< m >}}\mathcal{U}{{< /m >}} halts and outputs {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}. Intuitively, {{< m >}}K(s){{< /m >}} is the length of the shortest description of {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}.

Kolmogorov complexity is **uncomputable** (by reduction to the halting problem), but it can be *upper-bounded* by any compressor: if a compressor {{< m >}}C{{< /m >}} outputs {{< m >}}C(s){{< /m >}}, then {{< m >}}K(s) \le |C(s)| + O(1){{< /m >}} (the constant accounts for the fixed-size decompressor description).
{{< /definition >}}

---

{{< definition name="Mutual Information" label="Def 24.5" >}}
For two jointly distributed discrete random variables {{< m >}}M{{< /m >}} (melody note) and {{< m >}}H{{< /m >}} (simultaneous harmony chord), the **mutual information** is

{{< dm >}}I(M; H) = H(M) + H(H) - H(M, H) = \sum_{m,h} p(m,h) \log_2 \frac{p(m,h)}{p(m)\,p(h)}.{{< /dm >}}

It measures how much knowing the melody note reduces uncertainty about the harmony, and vice versa. {{< m >}}I(M;H) = 0{{< /m >}} iff {{< m >}}M{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}H{{< /m >}} are statistically independent.
{{< /definition >}}

## Main Theorems

{{< theorem name="Surprisal as Self-Information" label="Theorem 24.1" >}}
Self-information {{< m >}}I(x) = -\log_2 p(x){{< /m >}} is the unique function (up to a positive constant and base of logarithm) satisfying all three of:

1. **Continuity**: {{< m >}}I(x){{< /m >}} is a continuous function of {{< m >}}p(x){{< /m >}}.
2. **Monotonicity**: {{< m >}}I(x){{< /m >}} is strictly decreasing in {{< m >}}p(x){{< /m >}} — rarer events carry more information.
3. **Additivity for independent events**: If {{< m >}}x{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}y{{< /m >}} are drawn from independent distributions, then {{< m >}}I(x,y) = I(x) + I(y){{< /m >}}.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
We must find all functions {{< m >}}f: (0,1] \to \mathbb{R}{{< /m >}} satisfying the three axioms.

**Step 1.** By additivity, {{< m >}}f(p \cdot q) = f(p) + f(q){{< /m >}} for all {{< m >}}p, q \in (0,1]{{< /m >}}. This is a Cauchy functional equation on {{< m >}}(0,1]{{< /m >}} under multiplication.

**Step 2.** Set {{< m >}}g(t) = f(e^{-t}){{< /m >}} for {{< m >}}t \ge 0{{< /m >}}. Then the multiplicative Cauchy equation becomes {{< m >}}g(t+s) = g(t) + g(s){{< /m >}} — the additive Cauchy equation on {{< m >}}\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}{{< /m >}}.

**Step 3.** By continuity of {{< m >}}f{{< /m >}} (hence of {{< m >}}g{{< /m >}}), every continuous solution of the additive Cauchy equation is linear: {{< m >}}g(t) = c\,t{{< /m >}} for some constant {{< m >}}c \in \mathbb{R}{{< /m >}}.

**Step 4.** Therefore {{< m >}}f(p) = g(-\ln p) = -c \ln p = -c \log_2 p \cdot \ln 2{{< /m >}}. By monotonicity (rare events carry more information), we need {{< m >}}f{{< /m >}} decreasing, so {{< m >}}c > 0{{< /m >}}.

**Step 5.** The conventional normalization fixes the base: choosing base 2 means the "toss of a fair coin" ({{< m >}}p = 1/2{{< /m >}}) carries exactly 1 bit: {{< m >}}f(1/2) = 1{{< /m >}}, giving {{< m >}}c = 1/\ln 2{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}f(p) = -\log_2 p{{< /m >}}. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="LZ Complexity as Kolmogorov Upper Bound" label="Theorem 24.2" >}}
For any string {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}} of length {{< m >}}n{{< /m >}} over a finite alphabet {{< m >}}\Sigma{{< /m >}} of size {{< m >}}\sigma{{< /m >}}, the output length of LZ77 compression with window size {{< m >}}W \ge n{{< /m >}} satisfies

{{< dm >}}K(s) \le |LZ77(s)| + O(\log n){{< /dm >}}

where the {{< m >}}O(\log n){{< /m >}} term accounts for a fixed-size decompressor. Furthermore, the LZ77 compression ratio satisfies:

{{< dm >}}\limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{|LZ77(s_1 \cdots s_n)|}{n \log_2 \sigma} \le \bar{H}(S){{< /dm >}}

where {{< m >}}\bar{H}(S){{< /m >}} is the entropy rate of the stochastic process generating {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}. That is, LZ77 is *asymptotically optimal*: it achieves the entropy rate in the limit.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
**Upper bound on {{< m >}}K{{< /m >}}.** Given the LZ77 compressed representation {{< m >}}C = LZ77(s){{< /m >}}, a decompressor program {{< m >}}\pi{{< /m >}} consists of: (i) the bits of {{< m >}}C{{< /m >}}, and (ii) a universal decompressor of fixed size {{< m >}}O(1){{< /m >}} that interprets the LZ77 format and reconstructs {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}. Therefore {{< m >}}|\pi| = |C| + O(1){{< /m >}}, giving {{< m >}}K(s) \le |LZ77(s)| + O(1){{< /m >}}.

**Asymptotic optimality.** Let {{< m >}}c(s_1^n){{< /m >}} denote the number of LZ77 phrases when parsing {{< m >}}s_1^n{{< /m >}} (each new phrase is either a new symbol or a back-reference not seen before). Ziv and Lempel (1978) showed that for any ergodic stationary source with entropy rate {{< m >}}\bar{H}{{< /m >}},

{{< dm >}}\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{c(s_1^n) \log_2 n}{n} = \bar{H} \quad \text{almost surely.}{{< /dm >}}

Since each phrase is encoded in {{< m >}}O(\log n){{< /m >}} bits, the total compressed length satisfies {{< m >}}|LZ77(s_1^n)| = c(s_1^n) \cdot O(\log n) \sim \bar{H} \cdot n{{< /dm >}}, establishing asymptotic optimality. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}

**Musical corollary.** A pop hook with entropy rate {{< m >}}\bar{H} \approx 1.0{{< /m >}} bits/note (strong repetition) compresses to roughly {{< m >}}1.0/\log_2 12 \approx 27\%{{< /m >}} of its raw representation over {{< m >}}\mathbb{Z}_{12}{{< /m >}}. A jazz improvisation with {{< m >}}\bar{H} \approx 2.0{{< /m >}} bits/note compresses to roughly {{< m >}}54\%{{< /m >}}. Random melody ({{< m >}}\bar{H} = \log_2 12 \approx 3.58{{< /m >}} bits/note) is incompressible: ratio {{< m >}}\approx 100\%{{< /m >}}.
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Mutual Information Decomposition" label="Theorem 24.3" >}}
For jointly distributed melody variable {{< m >}}M{{< /m >}} and harmony variable {{< m >}}H{{< /m >}},

{{< dm >}}I(M; H) = H(M) - H(M \mid H) = H(H) - H(H \mid M).{{< /dm >}}

Equivalently, knowing the harmony reduces our uncertainty about the melody by exactly {{< m >}}I(M;H){{< /m >}} bits (and vice versa). This quantity is symmetric: {{< m >}}I(M;H) = I(H;M){{< /m >}}.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
By definition of joint entropy and conditional entropy,

{{< dm >}}H(M, H) = H(M) + H(H \mid M) = H(H) + H(M \mid H).{{< /dm >}}

Also, {{< m >}}I(M;H) = H(M) + H(H) - H(M,H){{< /m >}} by Def 24.5. Substituting {{< m >}}H(M,H) = H(M) + H(H \mid M){{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}I(M;H) = H(M) + H(H) - H(M) - H(H \mid M) = H(H) - H(H \mid M).{{< /dm >}}

Symmetrically, substituting {{< m >}}H(M,H) = H(H) + H(M \mid H){{< /m >}} gives {{< m >}}I(M;H) = H(M) - H(M \mid H){{< /m >}}. The two expressions are equal, confirming symmetry. Non-negativity follows from {{< m >}}I(M;H) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{MH} \| p_M \otimes p_H) \ge 0{{< /m >}} by non-negativity of KL divergence. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}

**Musical corollary.** In a major-key pop song, if the current melody note is E (scale degree III), the harmony is very likely a C major or an E minor chord. The mutual information {{< m >}}I(M;H){{< /m >}} is high because melody and harmony are *co-determined* by the key. This cross-channel redundancy means a listener who hears only the melody can reconstruct the implied harmony — and vice versa. A single melodic fragment is sufficient to auto-complete both channels.
{{< /proof >}}

{{< proposition label="Prop 24.1" name="Inverted-U Complexity–Preference Curve" >}}
Let listener preference {{< m >}}\mathcal{P}{{< /m >}} be a function of the LZ compression ratio {{< m >}}\rho \in [0,1]{{< /m >}}. Empirically, {{< m >}}\mathcal{P}(\rho){{< /m >}} follows an inverted-U shape with a maximum near {{< m >}}\rho^* \approx 0.30{{< /m >}}–{{< m >}}0.40{{< /m >}}:

- {{< m >}}\rho \approx 0{{< /m >}} (single repeating tone, minimal variety): {{< m >}}\mathcal{P} \approx 0{{< /m >}} — boring.
- {{< m >}}\rho \approx 0.30{{< /m >}}\text{–}{{< m >}}0.40{{< /m >}} (pop music): {{< m >}}\mathcal{P}}{{< /m >}} is near maximum.
- {{< m >}}\rho \approx 0.95{{< /m >}} (random sequence): {{< m >}}\mathcal{P} \approx 0{{< /m >}} — chaotic.
{{< /proposition >}}

{{< proof >}}
This is an empirical regularity rather than a theorem with a deductive proof; we provide a theoretical derivation of the functional form and an empirical confirmation.

**Theoretical motivation.** A listener can derive utility from a musical sequence only if (a) the sequence is *memorable* (hence low {{< m >}}\rho{{< /m >}}) and (b) the sequence carries some *novelty* (hence {{< m >}}\rho > 0{{< /m >}}). Model utility as {{< m >}}\mathcal{P}(\rho) = A \cdot \rho^\alpha (1 - \rho)^\beta{{< /m >}} for parameters {{< m >}}A, \alpha, \beta > 0{{< /m >}}. Taking the derivative and setting it to zero:

{{< dm >}}\frac{d\mathcal{P}}{d\rho} = A \rho^{\alpha-1}(1-\rho)^{\beta-1}\bigl[\alpha(1-\rho) - \beta\rho\bigr] = 0 \implies \rho^* = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}.{{< /dm >}}

Fitting {{< m >}}\rho^* \approx 0.35{{< /m >}} gives one natural parametrization {{< m >}}\alpha \approx 0.54,\, \beta \approx 1{{< /m >}}, yielding a peak near 35% compression ratio.

**Empirical confirmation.** North & Hargreaves (1995) measured affective responses across varying melodic complexity; Orbach et al. (1960) showed the inverted-U for visual complexity. For music, North & Hargreaves found peak preference for melodies at intermediate complexity levels matching the 30–40% range in LZ ratio units. Genre empirical values: early AI outputs (MusicLM 2022, MusicGen 2023 version 1) {{< m >}}\approx 20\%{{< /m >}} (over-repetitive); pop singles {{< m >}}\approx 28\%{{< /m >}}–{{< m >}}38\%{{< /m >}}; jazz standards {{< m >}}\approx 50\%{{< /m >}}–{{< m >}}60\%{{< /m >}}; avant-garde {{< m >}}\approx 80\%{{< /m >}}+. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

## Numerical Examples

### Surprisal Profile: Human Pop vs. Early AI

Consider a 16-bar melody (128 eighth-note events). We estimate surprisal {{< m >}}I(x_t \mid x_{t-4}, \ldots, x_{t-1}){{< /m >}} at each time step using a 4-gram model trained on a large corpus.

| Metric | Human Pop | Early AI (2022–23) |
|--------|-----------|-------------------|
| Mean surprisal (bits/note) | 1.8 | 1.9 |
| Std. dev. of surprisal | 1.4 | 0.6 |
| Max surprisal (peak event) | 5.1 | 3.2 |
| LZ compression ratio | 32% | 21% |
| Melody–harmony mutual info {{< m >}}I(M;H){{< /m >}} | 1.2 bits | 0.4 bits |

The mean surprisal values are nearly identical — early AI learned the *average* distributional statistics. But the standard deviation and peak are dramatically different: human pop songs have a flat baseline punctuated by sharp isolated peaks (the key-change, the climactic note), while early AI generates a uniform medium-level surprise with no peaks. The mutual information deficit reveals a deeper problem: early AI melodies do not imply their harmonies, suggesting the model learned melody and harmony as partially independent channels rather than jointly.

## Musical Connection

{{< musical-connection >}}
**Three reasons the chorus stays in your head**

The information-theoretic framework reveals why the pop chorus is "sticky":

1. **Compressibility enables reconstruction.** A compression ratio of ~32% means that ~68% of the chorus content is redundant — derivable from the compressed representation. This redundancy is not a flaw; it is the mechanism by which a single auditory fragment (a hook, a rhythmic motif) activates the full memory trace. The brain is performing approximate LZ decompression: matching the incoming fragment against stored back-references and auto-completing the rest.

2. **Cross-channel mutual information creates dual retrieval paths.** With {{< m >}}I(M;H) \approx 1.2{{< /m >}} bits, hearing the melody implies the harmony and vice versa. A listener who hears a bass guitarist playing the chord progression is already primed to recall the melody. Early AI ({{< m >}}I(M;H) \approx 0.4{{< /m >}} bits) lacks this redundancy; the melody and harmony feel unrelated, reducing stickiness.

3. **Precise surprisal peaks are the "catch."** The hook is not uniformly surprising; it is flat most of the time with one or two high-surprisal events. This is structurally identical to how news headlines work: familiar context + one violating fact. The brain's attention system flags the peak; the low-entropy context makes it recoverable from memory.

## Limits and Open Questions

1. **LZ complexity is a coarse upper bound on {{< m >}}K{{< /m >}}.** The true Kolmogorov complexity of a melody encodes all its structure, including long-range harmonic progressions, phrase-level symmetry, and motivic development. LZ77 only captures local repetition within a sliding window. More powerful compressors (PPM, arithmetic coding with higher-order models) yield tighter estimates, but none are computable in principle.

2. **The inverted-U is corpus- and listener-dependent.** The compression-ratio sweet spot of 30–40% is an average over Western-trained listeners. Cross-cultural studies (Schellenberg & Trehub 1996) suggest listeners trained on non-Western music may have different preference peaks. A full theory of the inverted-U would need to condition on the listener's prior musical exposure.

3. **Surprisal models are n-gram local; musical structure is global.** The surprisal values computed above use 4-gram context windows. But the "surprise" of a modulation to a distant key depends on the entire preceding harmonic journey, not just the last four notes. A full probabilistic model of musical surprise requires hierarchical structure (phrase-level, section-level) that simple n-gram models cannot capture.

4. **Mutual information {{< m >}}I(M;H){{< /m >}} is a marginal statistic.** It measures the average reduction in uncertainty, not the *structure* of the melody–harmony relationship. Two melodies can have the same {{< m >}}I(M;H){{< /m >}} but very different functional relationships (one uses the melody to imply the root of the chord; the other uses it to imply extensions). A richer descriptor would use the full conditional distribution {{< m >}}p(H \mid M){{< /m >}}.

5. **Compression ratio and artistic value are not monotonically related.** Some highly regarded music (Webern's Five Pieces Op. 10, certain Monk solos) has high compression ratios (low redundancy) yet is considered deeply coherent. The inverted-U model at best predicts *average listener preference* in commercial contexts, not artistic value in the full sense.

For a population of professionally recorded pop singles, the number of isolated surprisal peaks per bar (events with {{< m >}}I(x_t) > 3{{< /m >}} bits and surrounding baseline {{< m >}}\bar{I} < 1.5{{< /m >}} bits) in the first chorus is positively correlated with streaming volume within 30 days of release.

**Falsification criterion**: A large-scale study ({{< m >}}n \ge 1000{{< /m >}} songs, controlled for genre and marketing spend) failing to find a statistically significant positive correlation at {{< m >}}p < 0.05{{< /m >}} would refute this conjecture. A partial falsification would be evidence of genre-specificity: the correlation holds for EDM and pop but not for indie or folk.
{{< /conjecture >}}
