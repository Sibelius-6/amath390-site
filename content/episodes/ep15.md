## Overview

An opera singer has no microphone. A single voice must cut through 110 orchestral instruments in a live hall. This episode asks: how?

> *中文:* "答案藏在——3000赫兹。乐队能量在这里暴跌18分贝。这是歌手唯一的窗口——或者被淹没，或者穿透。"

The answer is not louder singing. It is **frequency targeting**: the singer concentrates acoustic energy precisely where the orchestra is weakest, where the human ear is most sensitive, and where the physics of the vocal tract allows a structural resonance enhancement.

This episode develops a three-layer quantitative model:

1. **Physical layer**: The orchestra's spectrum drops ~18 dB between its 500 Hz peak and the 3 kHz region. This is the frequency window.
2. **Source layer**: The singer's Formant — a cluster of vocal resonances {{< m >}}F_3{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}F_4{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}F_5{{< /m >}} near 2500–3500 Hz — raises the singer's output at exactly this window by approximately +10 dB.
3. **Perceptual layer**: The human auditory system (Fletcher-Munson equal-loudness curves, ISO 226) is ~10 dB more sensitive at 3 kHz than at 500 Hz at forte dynamics.

Combined, the physical signal-to-noise ratio is 48 dB; the perceptual penetration index reaches 49 dB (with a ±2 dB uncertainty on the Formant term). A fourth factor — room acoustics — modulates this advantage by up to ±7 dB depending on hall design.

The episode also presents spectral centroid measurements from four tenors, connecting vocal technique to quantifiable acoustic parameters.

## Definitions

{{< definition name="Acoustic Source-Filter Model" label="Definition 15.1" >}}
The **source-filter model** of voice production separates the voice into two independent stages:

1. **Source** (glottal excitation): The vocal folds produce a periodic buzz with fundamental frequency {{< m >}}f_0{{< /m >}} and a harmonic series at {{< m >}}f_0, 2f_0, 3f_0, \ldots{{< /m >}} The spectral envelope of the glottal source decays at approximately {{< m >}}-12{{< /m >}} dB/octave (i.e., the amplitude of harmonic {{< m >}}n{{< /m >}} is proportional to {{< m >}}1/n^2{{< /m >}}).

2. **Filter** (vocal tract): The air column above the larynx acts as an acoustic resonator. Its resonant frequencies {{< m >}}F_1, F_2, F_3, \ldots{{< /m >}} are called **formants**. The vocal tract transfer function amplifies harmonics near each formant and attenuates those between formants.

The radiated sound spectrum is:

{{< dm >}}|S(f)| = |G(f)| \cdot |H(f)| \cdot |R(f)|{{< /dm >}}

where {{< m >}}G(f){{< /m >}} is the glottal source spectrum, {{< m >}}H(f){{< /m >}} is the vocal tract transfer function, and {{< m >}}R(f){{< /m >}} is a radiation factor ({{< m >}}\propto f{{< /m >}}, adding +6 dB/octave to the final output).

Net source spectral slope: {{< m >}}-12 + 6 = -6{{< /m >}} dB/octave (before formant amplification).
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Formants and Vocal Tract Transfer Function" label="Definition 15.2" >}}
The **formant frequencies** {{< m >}}F_1, F_2, F_3, F_4, F_5{{< /m >}} are the resonant peaks of the vocal tract transfer function. For a simplified model of {{< m >}}k{{< /m >}} uncoupled resonators, the transfer function is:

{{< dm >}}H(f) = \prod_{k} \frac{1}{1 - (f/F_k)^2}{{< /dm >}}

Each term contributes a peak of amplitude {{< m >}}\rightarrow \infty{{< /m >}} at {{< m >}}f = F_k{{< /m >}} (in the lossless approximation). Including damping, each resonator has a finite bandwidth {{< m >}}B_k{{< /m >}} and the actual amplitude at resonance is {{< m >}}F_k / B_k{{< /m >}}.

Typical speech formant frequencies (male voice):

| Formant | Speech (Hz) | Opera singing (Hz) | Shift |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|-------|
| {{< m >}}F_1{{< /m >}} | 500 | 300–500 | varies |
| {{< m >}}F_2{{< /m >}} | 1500 | 800–1200 | varies |
| {{< m >}}F_3{{< /m >}} | 2200 | **2600** | +400 Hz |
| {{< m >}}F_4{{< /m >}} | 3100 | **3000** | −100 Hz |
| {{< m >}}F_5{{< /m >}} | 3900 | **3400** | −500 Hz |

{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Singer's Formant" label="Definition 15.3" >}}
The **Singer's Formant** (Sundberg, 1974) is a clustering of {{< m >}}F_3{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}F_4{{< /m >}}, and {{< m >}}F_5{{< /m >}} into a narrow frequency band near 2500–3500 Hz, producing an energy concentration approximately **+10 dB** above what would occur with the same formants in their speech positions.

This clustering is produced by a specific laryngeal configuration in which the pharyngeal cross-sectional area to laryngeal tube opening ratio reaches approximately **6:1**. This geometric ratio creates an additional coupled resonance mode that draws {{< m >}}F_3{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}F_4{{< /m >}}, and {{< m >}}F_5{{< /m >}} toward the same frequency region.

Measured range (Sundberg 1974): +8 to +12 dB. Canonical value: {{< m >}}\Delta_\text{Formant} \approx +10{{< /m >}} dB.

> *中文:* "这不是单一谐波，而是三个共振峰F3、F4、F5的聚合。"
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Equal-Loudness Contour (Fletcher-Munson / ISO 226)" label="Definition 15.4" >}}
An **equal-loudness contour** at {{< m >}}L{{< /m >}} phon is the curve of sound pressure level (SPL) in dB as a function of frequency {{< m >}}f{{< /m >}} such that a pure tone at {{< m >}}(f, \text{SPL}(f)){{< /m >}} is judged equally loud to a 1 kHz reference tone at {{< m >}}L{{< /m >}} dB SPL.

**Key data (ISO 226:2003)**:

| Level (phon) | SPL at 500 Hz (dB) | SPL at 3000 Hz (dB) | Advantage at 3 kHz |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | 45 | 38 | +7 dB |
| 60 | 60 | 53 | +7 dB |
| 80 | 77 | 68 | +9 dB |
| 100 | 98 | 88 | **+10 dB** |

At forte operatic dynamics (~90–100 phon), the ear requires 10 dB less SPL at 3 kHz than at 500 Hz to perceive the same loudness. Equivalently, a 3 kHz tone sounds 10 dB louder than a 500 Hz tone at the same physical SPL.

{{< m >}}\Delta_\text{Fletcher} \approx +10{{< /m >}} dB at operatic forte.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Spectral Centroid" label="Definition 15.5" >}}
The **spectral centroid** of a signal with power spectrum {{< m >}}P(f){{< /m >}} is:

{{< dm >}}f_c = \frac{\int_0^\infty f \cdot P(f)\, df}{\int_0^\infty P(f)\, df}{{< /dm >}}

In discrete form (DFT bins):

{{< dm >}}f_c = \frac{\sum_k f_k \cdot |X_k|^2}{\sum_k |X_k|^2}{{< /dm >}}

The spectral centroid is a single-number proxy for perceived **brightness** (timbre). Higher {{< m >}}f_c{{< /m >}} correlates with a "brighter," more "forward" vocal quality; lower {{< m >}}f_c{{< /m >}} with a "darker," "covered" sound.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Clarity Index C₈₀" label="Definition 15.6" >}}
The **clarity index** {{< m >}}C_{80}{{< /m >}} of a room measures the ratio of early to late reflected energy arriving at a listener position:

{{< dm >}}C_{80} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\int_0^{80\,\text{ms}} p^2(t)\, dt}{\int_{80\,\text{ms}}^\infty p^2(t)\, dt} \quad \text{(dB)}{{< /dm >}}

Higher {{< m >}}C_{80}{{< /m >}} (positive values) indicates more early energy — the room sounds cleaner and more "articulate." Lower {{< m >}}C_{80}{{< /m >}} (negative values) indicates excessive reverberation blurring onsets.

Representative values:
- Center orchestra (good hall): {{< m >}}C_{80} \approx +3{{< /m >}} dB
- Side balcony seat: {{< m >}}C_{80} \approx -2{{< /m >}} dB
- Difference: {{< m >}}\Delta C_{80} \approx 5{{< /m >}} dB of perceived clarity loss
{{< /definition >}}

## Main Theorems

{{< theorem name="Orchestra Spectral Attenuation Model" label="Theorem 15.1" >}}
The long-term average sound pressure level of a full symphony orchestra follows an approximate decay model:

{{< dm >}}\text{SPL}(f) \approx \text{SPL}_0 - \alpha \cdot \log_2\!\left(\frac{f}{f_0}\right){{< /dm >}}

with {{< m >}}\text{SPL}_0 = 80{{< /m >}} dB at the peak frequency {{< m >}}f_0 = 500{{< /m >}} Hz and {{< m >}}\alpha \approx 9{{< /m >}} dB/octave (Beranek 2004, Meyer 2009).

**Numerical values**:

| Frequency | Octaves from 500 Hz | Predicted SPL | Rounded |
|-----------|---------------------|--------------|---------|
| 1000 Hz | 1 | 71 dB | 71 dB |
| 2000 Hz | 2 | 62 dB | 62 dB |
| 3000 Hz | 2.585 | 56.7 dB | ~57 dB |
| 4000 Hz | 3 | 53 dB | 53 dB |

The **3 kHz window**: at 3000 Hz, orchestral SPL is approximately 57–62 dB (18–23 dB below peak), leaving a spectral gap that a well-trained singer can exploit.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
{{< m >}}\log_2(3000/500) = \log_2(6) = \ln 6 / \ln 2 \approx 1.7918 / 0.6931 \approx 2.585{{< /m >}}.

{{< m >}}\text{SPL}(3000) = 80 - 9 \times 2.585 = 80 - 23.3 \approx 56.7{{< /m >}} dB.

The narration uses 62 dB, which corresponds to {{< m >}}\alpha = 7{{< /m >}} dB/octave: {{< m >}}80 - 7 \times 2.585 \approx 62{{< /m >}} dB. Both values (7–9 dB/octave) appear in the literature depending on the ensemble and measurement method. We adopt the more conservative estimate {{< m >}}\text{SPL}(3000) \approx 62{{< /m >}} dB for the SNR calculation, consistent with the video. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Physical Signal-to-Noise Ratio" label="Theorem 15.2" >}}
When an opera singer performs at fortissimo dynamics with peak SPL of approximately 110 dB at 3 kHz (peak value; orchestra measured as long-term average), the **physical signal-to-noise ratio** at 3 kHz is:

{{< dm >}}\text{SNR}_\text{phys} = \text{SPL}_\text{singer}(3\,\text{kHz}) - \text{SPL}_\text{orchestra}(3\,\text{kHz}) = 110 - 62 = 48 \text{ dB}{{< /dm >}}

**Important caveat**: The singer's 110 dB is a peak SPL during fortissimo, while the orchestra's 62 dB is a long-term average. This "peak vs. average" comparison captures the perceptual reality that the singer's loudest moments cut through the orchestra's sustained texture.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< theorem name="Helmholtz Resonator Model of the Singer's Formant" label="Theorem 15.3" >}}
The pharynx-larynx system can be approximated as a **Helmholtz resonator**: a cavity of volume {{< m >}}V{{< /m >}} connected to the exterior through a neck of cross-sectional area {{< m >}}A{{< /m >}} and length {{< m >}}L{{< /m >}}. The resonant frequency is:

{{< dm >}}f_H = \frac{v}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{A}{V \cdot L}}{{< /dm >}}

where {{< m >}}v = 343{{< /m >}} m/s is the speed of sound.

Sundberg (1974) identified that when the ratio of pharyngeal cross-section to laryngeal tube opening reaches {{< m >}}\approx 6:1{{< /m >}}, the coupled resonance mode of the pharynx-larynx system places an extra resonance peak near 3000 Hz, drawing {{< m >}}F_3{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}F_4{{< /m >}}, and {{< m >}}F_5{{< /m >}} into proximity.

**Typical values** (Ternström 1988): {{< m >}}V \approx 35{{< /m >}} cm{{< m >}}^3{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}L \approx 2.5{{< /m >}} cm, {{< m >}}A{{< /m >}} tunable by laryngeal action. The 6:1 area ratio is a key degree of freedom controlled by the singer.

*Note*: The full vocal tract is a multi-cavity coupled system; the Helmholtz model is a pedagogical approximation. The mechanism is directionally correct but not quantitatively exact.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
Helmholtz resonance derivation (simplified): consider air in the neck as an acoustic mass {{< m >}}m = \rho L / A{{< /m >}} (where {{< m >}}\rho{{< /m >}} is air density), and the cavity volume as an acoustic compliance {{< m >}}C_V = V / (\rho v^2){{< /m >}}. The resonant frequency of this mass-spring system is:

{{< dm >}}f_H = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{m \cdot C_V}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{A / (\rho L)}{V / (\rho v^2)}} = \frac{v}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{A}{VL}}{{< /dm >}}

The 6:1 area ratio {{< m >}}A_\text{pharynx} : A_\text{larynx} = 6:1{{< /m >}} makes the laryngeal tube act as a Helmholtz neck relative to the pharyngeal cavity, positioning the resonance near 3 kHz for typical adult male vocal tract geometry. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Total Penetration Index" label="Theorem 15.4" >}}
Define the **perceptual penetration index** as the sum of the physical SNR and the relevant psychoacoustic advantages:

{{< dm >}}\text{PI} = \underbrace{\text{SNR}_\text{phys}}_\text{48 dB} + \underbrace{\Delta_\text{Formant}}_\text{+10 dB} + \underbrace{\Delta_\text{Fletcher}}_\text{+10 dB} \approx 49 \text{ dB}{{< /dm >}}

*Note: {{< m >}}\Delta_\text{Formant}{{< /m >}} is already incorporated in the singer's SPL figure if the 110 dB measurement includes the Formant clustering. In that interpretation, the 48 dB SNR already embeds the Formant effect, and {{< m >}}\Delta_\text{Fletcher} = +10{{< /m >}} dB is the pure perceptual bonus. The video presents all three layers separately for pedagogical clarity; the combined effect is approximately 49 dB.*

Under adverse conditions (suboptimal seat, insufficient technique):
{{< dm >}}\text{PI}_\text{adverse} = 49 - \underbrace{8}_\text{side seat} - \underbrace{10}_\text{no Formant} = 31 \text{ dB}{{< /dm >}}

approaching the ~20 dB empirical threshold below which singers are perceived as "masked."
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< theorem name="Sabine Reverberation Formula" label="Theorem 15.5" >}}
The **reverberation time** {{< m >}}T_{60}{{< /m >}} of a room — the time for a sound to decay 60 dB after the source stops — is given by **Sabine's formula** (1900):

{{< dm >}}T_{60} = \frac{0.161 \cdot V}{A}{{< /dm >}}

where:
- {{< m >}}V{{< /m >}} = room volume (m{{< m >}}^3{{< /m >}})
- {{< m >}}A = \sum_i \alpha_i S_i{{< /m >}} = total absorption area (m{{< m >}}^2{{< /m >}}), with {{< m >}}\alpha_i{{< /m >}} the absorption coefficient of surface {{< m >}}i{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}S_i{{< /m >}} its area
- The constant {{< m >}}0.161 = 4\ln(10^6)/v \approx 4 \times 13.816 / 343 \approx 0.161{{< /m >}} s/m in SI units

**Comparison of opera houses**:

| Hall | {{< m >}}T_{60}{{< /m >}} (mid-frequency) | Character |
|---|---|---|
| La Scala, Milan | 1.2 s | Clear, articulate |
| Glyndebourne | 1.3 s | Intimate |
| Royal Opera House | 1.4 s | Balanced |
| Vienna State Opera | 1.6 s | Rich |
| Sydney Opera House | 2.0 s | Reverberant, blurry |

{{< m >}}C_{80}{{< /m >}} difference between La Scala and Sydney: approximately 4 dB.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
Sabine's derivation assumes a diffuse sound field (energy uniformly distributed in all directions). The energy density {{< m >}}E(t){{< /m >}} in the room decays as:

{{< dm >}}\frac{dE}{dt} = -\frac{v \cdot A}{4V} E{{< /dm >}}

(each reflection removes a fraction {{< m >}}\bar{\alpha} = A/S_\text{total}{{< /m >}} of the energy hitting each surface). Solving:

{{< dm >}}E(t) = E_0 \exp\!\left(-\frac{v A}{4V} t\right){{< /dm >}}

Setting {{< m >}}E(t) = 10^{-6} E_0{{< /m >}} (60 dB decay, factor {{< m >}}10^6{{< /m >}} in power):

{{< dm >}}t = T_{60} = \frac{4V \ln(10^6)}{v A} = \frac{4V \times 6\ln 10}{v A} = \frac{24 \ln 10}{343} \cdot \frac{V}{A} \approx 0.161 \cdot \frac{V}{A} \quad \square{{< /dm >}}
{{< /proof >}}

## Bayreuth: Architectural Acoustics as Instrument

The Bayreuth Festspielhaus (1876, designed by Wagner) demonstrates that room design can substitute for vocal technique. Wagner submerged the orchestra pit below stage level and covered it with a curved hood that deflects orchestral sound toward the ceiling rather than directly toward the audience.

Effects:
1. The orchestra's direct sound to the audience is attenuated by approximately **7 dB** through the additional path length and diffraction losses.
2. The singer's direct sound arrives at the audience with no obstruction.
3. The **Haas effect** (precedence effect): when two sound sources arrive within 20–40 ms of each other, the auditory system fuses them and attributes localization to the earlier arrival. The singer's direct sound precedes the orchestra's reflected sound, so the voice is perceived as the dominant source regardless of amplitude relationships.

> *中文:* "瓦格纳把乐池沉入地下，弧形盖板把乐队声反射向天花板。歌手直达声先到，Haas效应让人声漂浮在乐队之上。乐队衰减7分贝——不靠歌手技术，靠建筑。"

## Failure Cases: Quantitative Analysis

### Lateral Seating (C80 Loss + High-Frequency Diffraction)

A seat at Ring 3 right side (example: seat B311 at the Canadian Opera Company house) experiences two independent losses compared to center orchestra:

**1. Early reflection deficit:**
{{< dm >}}\Delta C_{80} = C_{80}^\text{center} - C_{80}^\text{side} \approx (+3) - (-2) = 5 \text{ dB}{{< /dm >}}

Central seats receive multiple early reflections from lateral walls; side seats in enclosed boxes receive predominantly direct sound plus late reverberation, reducing intelligibility.

**2. High-frequency diffraction loss:**

At 3000 Hz: wavelength {{< m >}}\lambda = v/f = 343/3000 \approx 11{{< /m >}} cm. Objects of dimension {{< m >}}\sim 10{{< /m >}} cm or larger (seat backs, box partitions) shadow high frequencies effectively. Low frequencies (500 Hz, {{< m >}}\lambda \approx 69{{< /m >}} cm) diffract around these obstacles easily.

Estimated additional HF attenuation for obstructed sightlines: approximately **3 dB** at 3 kHz.

**Total lateral seat loss**: {{< m >}}5 + 3 = 8{{< /m >}} dB reduction in perceived vocal penetration.

### Technical Deficiency: Missing Formant Cluster

If the singer does not achieve the 6:1 pharynx-to-larynx area ratio (insufficient laryngeal lowering or pharyngeal expansion), {{< m >}}F_3{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}F_4{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}F_5{{< /m >}} remain at their speech positions (dispersed) rather than clustering near 3 kHz. The Formant gain of +10 dB is lost entirely.

### Combined adverse scenario:
{{< dm >}}\text{PI}_\text{worst} = 49 - 8 - 10 = 31 \text{ dB}{{< /dm >}}

If the room's {{< m >}}C_{80}{{< /m >}} is also unfavorable (e.g., {{< m >}}C_{80} < -3{{< /m >}} dB), the effective PI drops further, crossing the empirical ~20 dB masking threshold.

## Musical Connection

{{< musical-connection >}}
**The 3 kHz Window Across the Series**

The 3 kHz frequency region appears repeatedly across the arc:

- **EP02** (Fourier): The spectrum of a complex tone is the sum of harmonic components. The human voice's harmonic series above {{< m >}}f_0 \sim 200{{< /m >}} Hz reaches 3 kHz at approximately the 15th harmonic — well within the resonance-amplifiable range.

- **EP09** (Combination tones): The cochlea's nonlinear response generates combination tones at {{< m >}}|mf_1 - nf_2|{{< /m >}}. For a sung vowel with {{< m >}}F_1 \approx 700{{< /m >}} Hz and {{< m >}}F_2 \approx 1200{{< /m >}} Hz, combination products {{< m >}}2F_2 - F_1 \approx 1700{{< /m >}} Hz and {{< m >}}3F_2 - F_1 \approx 2900{{< /m >}} Hz fall near the Singer's Formant region.

- **EP15** (this episode): The Singer's Formant is the mechanism by which the vocalist intentionally concentrates energy in this region.

- **EP19** (vibrato): Vibrato-modulated formants create sidebands that sweep through the formant region, dynamically sampling it. The 3 kHz sensitivity window explains why vibrato rate (~5–7 Hz) interacts with formant frequency in ways that maximize perceived warmth.

- **EP28** (microphone physics): The condenser microphone models the diaphragm as a damped oscillator with resonant frequency in the 2–10 kHz range — the same region the Singer's Formant targets.

**Source-filter separation across instruments**

The source-filter decomposition (Definition 15.1) applies beyond the voice:

- **Violin**: The bowed string is the source; the body resonance modes are the filter.
- **Brass instruments**: The lip buzz is the source; the bell and bore are the filter.
- **Piano**: The hammer strike is the source; the soundboard is the filter.

The mathematical structure {{< m >}}S = G \cdot H \cdot R{{< /m >}} is universal. Opera singing is the case where the filter ({{< m >}}H{{< /m >}}, the vocal tract) is dynamically reconfigured by the performer in real time — a capability not available to instrument players.
{{< /musical-connection >}}

## Limits and Open Questions

1. **Sabine formula assumes diffuse field.** For non-diffuse rooms (long, narrow halls; rooms with strong focusing surfaces) Sabine's formula can overestimate {{< m >}}T_{60}{{< /m >}} by 20–50%. Eyring's formula {{< m >}}T_{60} = -0.161 V / [S \ln(1 - \bar{\alpha})]{{< /m >}} is more accurate for high-absorption environments.

2. **Masking threshold is not universal.** The empirical "~20 dB SNR for audibility" is a population average. Listeners with musical training show lower masking thresholds (better ability to segregate voices from noise). The cocktail party effect (auditory streaming) provides additional perceptual benefit beyond the physical SNR.

3. **Formant clustering mechanism needs clarification.** While Sundberg's 6:1 area ratio is widely cited, the precise biomechanical mechanism (arytenoid adduction, cricothyroid rotation, laryngeal lowering) remains debated. Recent laryngoscopic studies support multiple pathways to Singer's Formant production.

4. **Individual vocal tract geometry varies.** The 6:1 ratio produces a ~3 kHz resonance for an average adult male vocal tract. Sopranos have shorter vocal tracts; their Singer's Formant region is higher (~3.5–4 kHz). The model predicts this correctly: smaller {{< m >}}V{{< /m >}}, larger {{< m >}}f_H{{< /m >}} in the Helmholtz formula.

5. **Soprano formant tuning (not in EP15, but related).** At high soprano pitches ({{< m >}}f_0 > 700{{< /m >}} Hz), {{< m >}}F_1{{< /m >}} in normal vowels falls *below* {{< m >}}f_0{{< /m >}}. The soprano must retune {{< m >}}F_1{{< /m >}} to match {{< m >}}f_0{{< /m >}} to maintain acoustic efficiency — sacrificing vowel intelligibility for loudness. This is a frequency-domain trade-off studied by Joliver and Sundberg; it forward-connects to EP19 (vibrato and formant interaction).

{{< conjecture name="Optimal Formant Placement for Penetration" >}}
For a given orchestral texture, the singer's optimal formant cluster frequency {{< m >}}F^*{{< /m >}} is the frequency {{< m >}}f^*{{< /m >}} that maximizes:

{{< dm >}}f^* = \arg\max_f \left[ \text{SPL}_\text{singer}(f) - \text{SPL}_\text{orchestra}(f) + w(f) \right]{{< /dm >}}
