## Overview

Every recording engineer knows that swapping a microphone changes the sound — but why? The answer is not marketing, not mystique, and not simply "expensive = better." It is a second-order ordinary differential equation. The thin membrane inside any microphone is a **damped harmonic oscillator**: an elastic restoring force pulls it back, an inertial mass resists acceleration, and air damping bleeds away energy. These three forces combine into a single ODE whose solution determines everything you hear.

This episode derives that ODE from first principles, extracts the two dimensionless parameters that govern microphone character — natural frequency {{< m >}}\omega_0{{< /m >}} and quality factor {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} — and shows how the resulting transfer function {{< m >}}H(s){{< /m >}} produces the Bode-plot "fingerprint" of a microphone. We then add the two further dimensions of microphone design: polar pattern (which directions are captured) and transduction mechanism (displacement vs. velocity sensing). All three dimensions resolve to the same mathematical object: a transfer function, or a product of transfer functions.

> *中文:* "同一个歌手，同一句歌词，一支SM五十八，一支Neumann U八七。换了麦，像换了一个嗓子。这不是玄学，不是贵的就更好，是物理。"

## Definitions

{{< definition name="Damped Harmonic Oscillator" label="Definition 28.1" >}}
Let {{< m >}}x(t){{< /m >}} denote the displacement of a membrane of mass {{< m >}}m > 0{{< /m >}}, subject to an elastic restoring coefficient {{< m >}}k > 0{{< /m >}}, a viscous damping coefficient {{< m >}}c \geq 0{{< /m >}}, and an external forcing {{< m >}}F(t){{< /m >}}. Newton's second law gives the **damped harmonic oscillator ODE**:

{{< dm >}}m\,\ddot{x} + c\,\dot{x} + k\,x = F(t){{< /dm >}}

The **natural (angular) frequency** is

{{< dm >}}\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}{{< /dm >}}

and the **damping ratio** is

{{< dm >}}\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{mk}} = \frac{c}{2m\omega_0}{{< /dm >}}

Dividing through by {{< m >}}m{{< /m >}} and writing {{< m >}}f(t) = F(t)/m{{< /m >}}, the ODE becomes the standard form:

{{< dm >}}\ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot{x} + \omega_0^2\,x = f(t){{< /dm >}}
{{< /definition >}}

**Worked example.** A condenser capsule has a membrane of effective mass {{< m >}}m = 0.5 \times 10^{-6}{{< /m >}} kg and elastic stiffness {{< m >}}k = 200{{< /m >}} N/m. Then {{< m >}}\omega_0 = \sqrt{200 / (0.5 \times 10^{-6})} = \sqrt{4 \times 10^8} \approx 2 \times 10^4{{< /m >}} rad/s, corresponding to {{< m >}}f_0 = \omega_0 / (2\pi) \approx 3.18{{< /m >}} kHz — right in the presence band exploited by the singer's formant from {{< episode-ref ep="15" >}}EP15{{< /episode-ref >}}.

{{< definition name="Quality Factor Q" label="Definition 28.2" >}}
The **quality factor** (Q factor) of a damped oscillator is defined as

{{< dm >}}Q = \frac{1}{2\zeta} = \frac{m\omega_0}{c} = \frac{\sqrt{mk}}{c}{{< /dm >}}

Physically, {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} measures the ratio of energy stored to energy dissipated per radian of oscillation. Three regimes:

- {{< m >}}Q < \tfrac{1}{2}{{< /m >}} (i.e. {{< m >}}\zeta > 1{{< /m >}}): **overdamped** — returns to equilibrium without oscillating
- {{< m >}}Q = \tfrac{1}{2}{{< /m >}} (i.e. {{< m >}}\zeta = 1{{< /m >}}): **critically damped** — fastest non-oscillatory return
- {{< m >}}Q > \tfrac{1}{2}{{< /m >}} (i.e. {{< m >}}\zeta < 1{{< /m >}}): **underdamped** — rings at angular frequency {{< m >}}\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}{{< /m >}}; a gain peak appears in the frequency response
{{< /definition >}}

**Worked example.** The Shure SM58 has {{< m >}}Q \approx 1.5{{< /m >}}, so {{< m >}}\zeta = 1/(2 \times 1.5) = 1/3 \approx 0.333{{< /m >}}. Since {{< m >}}\zeta < 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707{{< /m >}}, the SM58 membrane is underdamped and exhibits a gain peak — but with low {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} the peak is broad and the response stays nearly flat across the vocal range. The Neumann U87 has {{< m >}}Q \approx 3{{< /m >}} to {{< m >}}5{{< /m >}}, giving a narrow bright peak near 10 kHz.

{{< definition name="Transfer Function" label="Definition 28.3" >}}
Taking the Laplace transform of the standard-form ODE (with zero initial conditions), and denoting the Laplace variable by {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}, the **transfer function** of the damped harmonic oscillator is

{{< dm >}}H(s) = \frac{X(s)}{F(s)} = \frac{\omega_0^2}{s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0\,s + \omega_0^2}{{< /dm >}}

The **frequency response** is obtained by substituting {{< m >}}s = i\omega{{< /m >}} (where {{< m >}}i = \sqrt{-1}{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}\omega{{< /m >}} is the real angular frequency):

{{< dm >}}H(i\omega) = \frac{\omega_0^2}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) + 2i\zeta\omega_0\omega}{{< /dm >}}

The magnitude response (Bode gain plot) is

{{< dm >}}|H(i\omega)| = \frac{\omega_0^2}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2\omega_0^2\omega^2}}{{< /dm >}}
{{< /definition >}}

**Worked example.** At {{< m >}}\omega = 0{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}|H| = 1{{< /m >}} (0 dB, unity gain at DC). At {{< m >}}\omega = \omega_0{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}|H(i\omega_0)| = \omega_0^2 / (2\zeta\omega_0^2) = 1/(2\zeta) = Q{{< /m >}}. So the on-resonance gain equals the Q factor in linear units, or {{< m >}}20\log_{10}Q{{< /m >}} dB. For {{< m >}}Q = 3{{< /m >}}: gain at resonance = 9.5 dB.

The following script generates Bode magnitude plots contrasting a broad SM58 response (Q ≈ 1.5) with the sharper U87 presence peak (Q ≈ 4), overlaid with the singer's formant reference from EP15:

```python
# Figure: Bode magnitude plot — SM58 (Q≈1.5) vs Neumann U87 (Q≈4)
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def bode_magnitude(omega, omega0, zeta):
    """|H(iω)| for second-order underdamped system."""
    s = 1j * omega
    denom = (s/omega0)**2 + 2*zeta*(s/omega0) + 1
    return np.abs(1 / denom)

f = np.geomspace(20, 20000, 1000)
omega = 2 * np.pi * f

# SM58: omega0 ~ 8 kHz, Q = 1.5  → zeta = 1/(2*1.5) ≈ 0.33
omega0_sm58 = 2 * np.pi * 8000
zeta_sm58   = 1 / (2 * 1.5)
H_sm58 = bode_magnitude(omega, omega0_sm58, zeta_sm58)

# U87: omega0 ~ 10 kHz, Q = 4 → zeta = 0.125
omega0_u87 = 2 * np.pi * 10000
zeta_u87   = 1 / (2 * 4.0)
H_u87 = bode_magnitude(omega, omega0_u87, zeta_u87)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4))
ax.semilogx(f, 20*np.log10(H_sm58), 'b-', lw=2, label='SM58  (Q≈1.5, f₀≈8 kHz) — broad, flat')
ax.semilogx(f, 20*np.log10(H_u87),  'r-', lw=2, label='U87   (Q≈4.0, f₀≈10 kHz) — bright peak')
ax.axvline(3000, ls=':', color='green', alpha=0.7, label="Singer's formant ~3 kHz (EP15)")
ax.set_xlabel('Frequency (Hz)')
ax.set_ylabel('|H(iω)| (dB)')
ax.set_title("Bode Magnitude Plot — Microphone Transfer Functions")
ax.legend(fontsize=9)
ax.grid(True, which='both', alpha=0.3)
ax.set_xlim(20, 20000)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

{{< definition name="Polar Pattern" label="Definition 28.4" >}}
The directional sensitivity pattern of a microphone is described by the **polar pattern function**

{{< dm >}}P(\theta) = a + (1-a)\cos\theta, \quad \theta \in [0, 2\pi){{< /dm >}}

where {{< m >}}a \in [0, 1]{{< /m >}} is the omnidirectional component weight. Special cases:

| {{< m >}}a{{< /m >}} | Name | On-axis (0°) | Rear (180°) | Null |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Omnidirectional | 1 | 1 | none |
| 0.5 | Cardioid | 1 | 0 | 180° |
| 0 | Figure-8 (bidirectional) | 1 | −1 | ±90° |
| 0.37 | Supercardioid | 1 | −0.26 | ±125° |

The cardioid pattern is realised physically by combining a pressure sensor (omnidirectional) with a pressure-gradient sensor (figure-8) and summing their outputs with equal weight.
{{< /definition >}}

The following script generates polar diagrams for all three canonical patterns — omnidirectional, cardioid, and figure-8 — directly from the formula {{< m >}}P(\theta) = a + (1-a)\cos\theta{{< /m >}}:

```python
# Figure: Microphone polar patterns — omnidirectional, cardioid, figure-8
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

theta = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 360)

def polar_pattern(theta, a):
    """P(θ) = a + (1-a)·cos(θ). Values clipped to 0 (no negative gain)."""
    return np.maximum(a + (1 - a) * np.cos(theta), 0)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, subplot_kw={'projection': 'polar'}, figsize=(11, 4))
configs = [(1.0, 'Omnidirectional\n(a = 1)', '#3498db'),
           (0.5, 'Cardioid\n(a = 0.5)', '#e74c3c'),
           (0.0, 'Figure-8\n(a = 0)',   '#27ae60')]

for ax, (a, title, color) in zip(axes, configs):
    r = polar_pattern(theta, a)
    ax.plot(theta, r, color=color, lw=2)
    ax.fill(theta, r, color=color, alpha=0.2)
    ax.set_title(title, pad=14, fontsize=10)
    ax.set_ylim(0, 1.1)
    ax.set_rticks([0.5, 1.0])
    ax.set_rlabel_position(45)
    # Mark front (0°) and back (180°)
    ax.annotate('front', xy=(0, 1.1), ha='center', fontsize=8, color='gray')
    ax.annotate('back',  xy=(np.pi, 1.1), ha='center', fontsize=8, color='gray')

plt.suptitle("Polar Patterns: P(θ) = a + (1−a)·cos(θ)", y=1.02)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Main Theorems

{{< theorem name="Resonance Peak Condition" label="Theorem 28.1" >}}
For the standard second-order transfer function {{< m >}}H(i\omega) = \omega_0^2 / [(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) + 2i\zeta\omega_0\omega]{{< /m >}}, a local maximum of {{< m >}}|H(i\omega)|{{< /m >}} in {{< m >}}\omega > 0{{< /m >}} exists **if and only if** {{< m >}}\zeta < 1/\sqrt{2}{{< /m >}}. When it exists, the peak occurs at

{{< dm >}}\omega_{\text{peak}} = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}{{< /dm >}}

and the peak gain is

{{< dm >}}|H(i\omega_{\text{peak}})| = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}{{< /dm >}}
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
Let {{< m >}}u = \omega^2{{< /m >}} and define

{{< dm >}}D(u) = (\omega_0^2 - u)^2 + 4\zeta^2\omega_0^2\,u{{< /dm >}}

so that {{< m >}}|H(i\omega)|^2 = \omega_0^4 / D(u){{< /m >}}. Maximising {{< m >}}|H|{{< /m >}} is equivalent to minimising {{< m >}}D(u){{< /m >}}.

Differentiate with respect to {{< m >}}u{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}\frac{dD}{du} = -2(\omega_0^2 - u) + 4\zeta^2\omega_0^2{{< /dm >}}

Setting {{< m >}}dD/du = 0{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}2(\omega_0^2 - u) = 4\zeta^2\omega_0^2 \implies u^* = \omega_0^2(1 - 2\zeta^2){{< /dm >}}

Since we require {{< m >}}u^* = \omega_{\text{peak}}^2 > 0{{< /m >}}, a peak exists only when {{< m >}}1 - 2\zeta^2 > 0{{< /m >}}, i.e. {{< m >}}\zeta < 1/\sqrt{2}{{< /m >}}.

Substituting {{< m >}}\omega_{\text{peak}} = \omega_0\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}{{< /m >}} back into {{< m >}}D{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}D(u^*) = (2\zeta^2\omega_0^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2\omega_0^2 \cdot \omega_0^2(1-2\zeta^2) = 4\zeta^2\omega_0^4(1 - \zeta^2){{< /dm >}}

Therefore

{{< dm >}}|H(i\omega_{\text{peak}})| = \frac{\omega_0^2}{\sqrt{4\zeta^2\omega_0^4(1-\zeta^2)}} = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}{{< /dm >}}

The second derivative {{< m >}}d^2D/du^2 = 2 > 0{{< /m >}} confirms this is a minimum of {{< m >}}D{{< /m >}}, hence a maximum of {{< m >}}|H|{{< /m >}}. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Laplace Derivation of the Transfer Function" label="Theorem 28.2" >}}
Starting from the standard-form ODE

{{< dm >}}\ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot{x} + \omega_0^2\,x = f(t){{< /dm >}}

with zero initial conditions {{< m >}}x(0) = \dot{x}(0) = 0{{< /m >}}, the Laplace transform yields

{{< dm >}}H(s) = \frac{\mathcal{L}\{x\}}{\mathcal{L}\{f\}} = \frac{\omega_0^2}{s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0 s + \omega_0^2}{{< /dm >}}

The two poles are located at {{< m >}}s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm i\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}{{< /m >}}, and lie in the left half-plane (system is stable) if and only if {{< m >}}\zeta > 0{{< /m >}}.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
Apply the Laplace transform linearity and the differentiation rule {{< m >}}\mathcal{L}\{\ddot{x}\} = s^2 X(s) - s\,x(0) - \dot{x}(0){{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}\mathcal{L}\{\dot{x}\} = s X(s) - x(0){{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}[s^2 X(s) - 0 - 0] + 2\zeta\omega_0[s X(s) - 0] + \omega_0^2 X(s) = F(s){{< /dm >}}

Factor {{< m >}}X(s){{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}X(s)\bigl(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0 s + \omega_0^2\bigr) = F(s){{< /dm >}}

Dividing both sides by {{< m >}}(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0 s + \omega_0^2){{< /m >}} and noting that the denominator factors as {{< m >}}(s - s_1)(s - s_2){{< /m >}} with roots from the quadratic formula gives

{{< dm >}}s_{1,2} = \frac{-2\zeta\omega_0 \pm \sqrt{4\zeta^2\omega_0^2 - 4\omega_0^2}}{2} = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm i\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}{{< /dm >}}

Real part of each pole is {{< m >}}-\zeta\omega_0 < 0{{< /m >}} for {{< m >}}\zeta > 0{{< /m >}}, confirming BIBO stability. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Dynamic vs Condenser Frequency Response Ratio" label="Theorem 28.3" >}}
A **condenser microphone** senses membrane displacement {{< m >}}x(t){{< /m >}}; its output is proportional to {{< m >}}X(s){{< /m >}} in the Laplace domain. A **dynamic (moving-coil) microphone** senses membrane velocity {{< m >}}\dot{x}(t){{< /m >}}; its output is proportional to {{< m >}}s\,X(s){{< /m >}}.

Their frequency responses along the frequency axis {{< m >}}s = i\omega{{< /m >}} differ by a factor of {{< m >}}i\omega{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}\frac{H_{\text{dyn}}(i\omega)}{H_{\text{cond}}(i\omega)} = i\omega{{< /dm >}}

In magnitude: {{< m >}}|H_{\text{dyn}}(i\omega)| = \omega \cdot |H_{\text{cond}}(i\omega)|{{< /dm >}}.

Consequently, relative to the displacement response, the velocity (dynamic) response rises at +6 dB/octave at low frequencies and falls at −6 dB/octave at high frequencies above resonance.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
The induced EMF in a dynamic microphone coil moving through a magnetic field of strength {{< m >}}B{{< /m >}} with effective wire length {{< m >}}L{{< /m >}} is

{{< dm >}}V(t) = BL\,\dot{x}(t){{< /dm >}}

Taking the Laplace transform: {{< m >}}\mathcal{L}\{V\} = BL \cdot s\,X(s){{< /m >}} (using the differentiation rule). The transfer function from forcing {{< m >}}f(t){{< /m >}} to output voltage is therefore

{{< dm >}}H_{\text{dyn}}(s) = \frac{BL \cdot s\,X(s)}{F(s)} = BL \cdot s \cdot H_{\text{cond}}(s){{< /dm >}}

Evaluating on the imaginary axis: {{< m >}}H_{\text{dyn}}(i\omega) = BL \cdot (i\omega) \cdot H_{\text{cond}}(i\omega){{< /dm >}}.

Taking magnitudes and ignoring the constant {{< m >}}BL{{< /m >}}: {{< m >}}|H_{\text{dyn}}(i\omega)| = \omega \cdot |H_{\text{cond}}(i\omega)|{{< /dm >}}.

The factor {{< m >}}\omega{{< /m >}} corresponds to a slope of {{< m >}}+20{{< /m >}} dB/decade = {{< m >}}+6{{< /m >}} dB/octave in the pass-band below resonance. Above resonance the denominator of {{< m >}}H_{\text{cond}}{{< /m >}} grows as {{< m >}}\omega^2{{< /m >}}, so {{< m >}}|H_{\text{dyn}}| \propto \omega / \omega^2 = 1/\omega{{< /m >}}, i.e. {{< m >}}-6{{< /m >}} dB/octave. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

## Numerical Examples

**SM58 peak location.** The Shure SM58 has {{< m >}}Q \approx 1.5{{< /m >}}, so {{< m >}}\zeta = 1/(2Q) = 1/3{{< /m >}}. The condition for a peak is {{< m >}}\zeta < 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707{{< /m >}}: satisfied. Peak location relative to natural frequency:

{{< dm >}}\frac{\omega_{\text{peak}}}{\omega_0} = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} = \sqrt{1 - 2/9} = \sqrt{7/9} = \frac{\sqrt{7}}{3} \approx 0.882{{< /dm >}}

So the SM58 peak sits about 12% below the natural frequency — the resonance is pulled slightly down by damping. Peak gain:

{{< dm >}}|H_{\text{peak}}| = \frac{1}{2 \cdot (1/3) \cdot \sqrt{1 - 1/9}} = \frac{1}{(2/3)\sqrt{8/9}} = \frac{1}{(2/3)(2\sqrt{2}/3)} = \frac{9}{4\sqrt{2}} \approx 1.59{{< /dm >}}

That is {{< m >}}20\log_{10}(1.59) \approx 4.0{{< /m >}} dB — a gentle broad hump.

**Neumann U87 peak sharpness.** With {{< m >}}Q = 4{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}\zeta = 0.125{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}\omega_{\text{peak}} = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - 2(0.125)^2} = \omega_0\sqrt{0.969} \approx 0.984\,\omega_0{{< /dm >}}

Peak gain: {{< m >}}|H_{\text{peak}}| \approx 1/(2 \times 0.125 \times \sqrt{1-0.0156}) \approx 4.03{{< /dm >}}, i.e. about 12.1 dB — a clearly audible narrow brightness spike near {{< m >}}\omega_0 \approx 2\pi \times 10\,000{{< /m >}} rad/s.

**Polar pattern: cardioid front-to-back ratio.** With {{< m >}}a = 0.5{{< /m >}}, the front ({{< m >}}\theta = 0{{< /m >}}) gain is {{< m >}}P(0) = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1{{< /m >}}. The rear ({{< m >}}\theta = \pi{{< /m >}}) gain is {{< m >}}P(\pi) = 0.5 - 0.5 = 0{{< /m >}}: perfect rear null. The −3 dB point occurs where {{< m >}}P(\theta) = 1/\sqrt{2}{{< /m >}}: {{< m >}}0.5 + 0.5\cos\theta = 1/\sqrt{2}{{< /m >}}, giving {{< m >}}\cos\theta = \sqrt{2}-1 \approx 0.414{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}\theta \approx 65.5°{{< /m >}}.

**Formant interaction.** The singer's formant from {{< episode-ref ep="15" >}}EP15{{< /episode-ref >}} concentrates vocal energy near 3 kHz. If a microphone's {{< m >}}\omega_0 = 2\pi \times 10\,000{{< /m >}} rad/s but with a broad {{< m >}}Q=1.5{{< /m >}} skirt extending down to 3 kHz, the microphone gain at 3 kHz is (using the transfer function magnitude formula with {{< m >}}\omega/\omega_0 = 0.3{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}\zeta=1/3{{< /m >}}):

{{< dm >}}|H(i \cdot 0.3\omega_0)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-0.09)^2 + 4(1/9)(0.09)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.829 + 0.04}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.869}} \approx 1.07{{< /dm >}}

About 0.6 dB lift — modest, but stacked on top of the singer's formant, the combined effect is a noticeable presence boost.

## Musical Connection

{{< musical-connection >}}
**Choosing a microphone is choosing a transfer function product.**

Every microphone in the signal chain adds its transfer function {{< m >}}H_{\text{mic}}(i\omega){{< /m >}} to the vocal transfer function {{< m >}}H_{\text{voice}}(i\omega){{< /m >}}. What arrives at the preamp is the product {{< m >}}H_{\text{voice}} \cdot H_{\text{mic}}{{< /m >}}. The engineer's intuitive vocabulary — "presence," "air," "warmth," "proximity effect" — maps directly onto the shape of {{< m >}}|H_{\text{mic}}(i\omega)|{{< /m >}}:

- **Presence** = a mid-high peak (3–8 kHz), aligning with the singer's formant from {{< episode-ref ep="15" >}}EP15{{< /episode-ref >}}
- **Air** = a narrow high-frequency peak above 10 kHz (high {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}})
- **Warmth** = a low-frequency shoulder below 200 Hz (low {{< m >}}\omega_0{{< /m >}})
- **Proximity effect** = a dynamic microphone's inherent bass boost when used close-up, arising from near-field pressure-gradient enhancement of the velocity signal

The SM58/U87 contrast encodes the engineering trade-off: high {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} gives distinctive character but requires careful placement; low {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} is forgiving but generic. Neither is "better" — they are different poles in the complex {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}-plane.

**Connection forward.** In {{< episode-ref ep="29" >}}EP29{{< /episode-ref >}}, the Helmholtz resonator that absorbs low-frequency room modes is governed by the identical ODE with a different physical interpretation of {{< m >}}m{{< /m >}} (air mass in the neck) and {{< m >}}k{{< /m >}} (restoring compressibility of cavity air). In {{< episode-ref ep="37" >}}EP37{{< /episode-ref >}}, the biquad EQ filter is the digital implementation of the same transfer function — {{< m >}}H(s){{< /m >}} discretised via the bilinear transform.

## Limits and Open Questions

1. **Membrane non-linearity.** The ODE of Definition 28.1 assumes a linear elastic restoring force (Hooke's law). Real membranes at high sound pressure levels exhibit stiffness hardening ({{< m >}}k \to k + \alpha x^2{{< /m >}}), producing even-harmonic distortion. This is the origin of microphone THD specifications and is not captured by the linear transfer function.

2. **Distributed membrane modes.** The lumped-mass model treats the membrane as a point mass. A real circular membrane (governed by the two-dimensional wave equation in polar coordinates) has infinitely many normal modes — Bessel functions {{< m >}}J_n(k_{nm} r){{< /m >}} — each contributing a separate resonance peak. The transfer function {{< m >}}H(s){{< /m >}} of Definition 28.3 approximates only the dominant lowest mode.

3. **Proximity effect and directional microphones.** The pressure-gradient (figure-8) component of a cardioid pattern is inherently a near-field-dependent signal. At close distances the pressure-gradient term grows as {{< m >}}1/r^2{{< /m >}} rather than {{< m >}}1/r{{< /m >}}, causing a bass boost that is not captured by the far-field polar pattern {{< m >}}P(\theta){{< /m >}}. A complete model requires a two-variable transfer function {{< m >}}H(i\omega, r){{< /m >}}.

4. **Self-noise and thermal noise floor.** The Fluctuation-Dissipation theorem predicts that the same damping coefficient {{< m >}}c{{< /m >}} that determines {{< m >}}\zeta{{< /m >}} also sets the Nyquist thermal noise force {{< m >}}S_F = 4k_B T c{{< /m >}}. High {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} (low {{< m >}}c{{< /m >}}) microphones therefore have lower self-noise — a direct physical link between frequency response shape and noise floor specification.

5. **Vector cardioid and coincident-pair stereo.** The polar pattern {{< m >}}P(\theta) = a + (1-a)\cos\theta{{< /m >}} is a scalar function. Real coincident microphone arrays (XY, ORTF, Mid-Side) multiply polar patterns across multiple capsules to synthesise arbitrary stereo images. The mathematical framework is the **spherical harmonic decomposition** of the sound field, connecting back to the eigenfunction expansions of {{< episode-ref ep="2" >}}EP02{{< /episode-ref >}}.

{{< conjecture name="Optimal Q for Formant Alignment" >}}
For a vocalist whose singer's formant peak is centred at frequency {{< m >}}f_F{{< /m >}} (typically 2.8–3.2 kHz), there exists an optimal microphone quality factor {{< m >}}Q^*{{< /m >}} such that the composite response {{< m >}}|H_{\text{voice}}(f)| \cdot |H_{\text{mic}}(f)|{{< /m >}} maximises perceived intelligibility as measured by the Speech Intelligibility Index (ANSI S3.5). The conjecture is that {{< m >}}Q^*{{< /m >}} depends on the vocalist's individual formant bandwidth, implying that "microphone matching" is a speaker-dependent optimisation problem with no universal answer. **Falsification criterion**: measure STI scores for 20 vocalists × 5 microphones in an anechoic chamber; test whether the highest-scoring microphone per vocalist can be predicted from the vocal formant bandwidth alone.
{{< /conjecture >}}
