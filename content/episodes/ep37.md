## Overview

Turn any knob on your DAW's parametric EQ, and somewhere inside the plugin, five floating-point numbers change. Those five numbers — {{< m >}}b_0, b_1, b_2, a_1, a_2{{< /m >}} — are the coefficients of a **biquad filter**, and they completely determine two things: where the poles and zeros sit in the complex {{< m >}}z{{< /m >}}-plane, and therefore what the frequency response looks like. The insight of this episode is that these two descriptions — "five coefficients" and "pole-zero constellation" — are the same object in two different languages.

A biquad is the smallest non-trivial building block of digital signal processing: a second-order IIR (infinite impulse response) filter whose transfer function is a ratio of degree-2 polynomials in {{< m >}}z^{-1}{{< /m >}}. Every EQ band in a professional plug-in is one biquad. A five-band parametric EQ chains five biquads in series. The microphone from {{< episode-ref ep="28" >}}EP28{{< /episode-ref >}} is, after discretisation, also a biquad — its diaphragm resonance becomes a conjugate pole pair sitting just inside the unit circle.

Building a digital biquad from an analog prototype (Butterworth, Chebyshev, etc.) requires mapping the continuous {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}-plane to the discrete {{< m >}}z{{< /m >}}-plane. The standard industrial method is the **bilinear transform**: replace {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}} by the rational function {{< m >}}\tfrac{2}{T}\tfrac{1-z^{-1}}{1+z^{-1}}{{< /m >}}. This map sends the imaginary axis bijectively onto the unit circle, preserving stability. Its one cost is a non-linear compression of the frequency axis — **frequency warping** — which must be pre-compensated during design.

The Audio EQ Cookbook (Bristow-Johnson, 1994/2016) collects closed-form formulas that absorb the bilinear algebra and expose only the three musically natural parameters: center frequency {{< m >}}f_0{{< /m >}}, gain {{< m >}}G{{< /m >}} (in dB), and bandwidth {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}}. Raising {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} narrows the peak and pushes the poles toward the unit circle; raising {{< m >}}G{{< /m >}} expands the pole magnitude slightly outward. As {{< m >}}Q \to \infty{{< /m >}} the poles touch the unit circle and the filter becomes marginally unstable.

This episode also introduces **dynamic range compression**, which operates not in the frequency domain but in the amplitude domain. A compressor is a time-varying gain {{< m >}}g(t){{< /m >}} whose value is estimated by a first-order IIR envelope follower. The attack and release time constants correspond directly to the coefficient {{< m >}}\alpha = e^{-1/(f_s \tau)}{{< /m >}} of that one-pole filter.

> *中文:* "在复平面上，有两个点在缓慢移动。你转动均衡器的旋钮，它们就漂移——离单位圆越近，某个频率就被放大越多。这两个点叫做极点。今天我们来看清楚：你的EQ插件，本质上是在移动复平面上的极点。"

## Definitions

{{< definition name="Biquad Transfer Function" label="Definition 37.1" >}}
A **biquad** (second-order IIR) filter is defined by five real coefficients {{< m >}}b_0, b_1, b_2, a_1, a_2 \in \mathbb{R}{{< /m >}}. Its **transfer function** in the {{< m >}}z{{< /m >}}-domain is

{{< dm >}}H(z) = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}}{{< /dm >}}

Multiplying numerator and denominator by {{< m >}}z^2{{< /m >}} gives the equivalent polynomial form:

{{< dm >}}H(z) = \frac{b_0 z^2 + b_1 z + b_2}{z^2 + a_1 z + a_2}{{< /dm >}}

The **zeros** of {{< m >}}H(z){{< /m >}} are the roots of the numerator polynomial; the **poles** are the roots of the denominator polynomial. The **difference equation** (time-domain implementation) is

{{< dm >}}y[n] = b_0\,x[n] + b_1\,x[n-1] + b_2\,x[n-2] \;-\; a_1\,y[n-1] - a_2\,y[n-2]{{< /dm >}}

The system requires two past input samples and two past output samples — four numbers of state.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Stability of a Biquad" label="Definition 37.2" >}}
A biquad is **BIBO stable** (bounded-input bounded-output stable) if and only if both poles lie strictly inside the unit circle:

{{< dm >}}|z_{\text{pole}}| < 1{{< /dm >}}

For a real-coefficient biquad the poles are either both real, or form a **complex-conjugate pair** {{< m >}}z_{1,2} = r\,e^{\pm i\theta}{{< /m >}} with {{< m >}}r = \sqrt{a_2}{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}\theta = \arccos\!\bigl(-a_1/(2\sqrt{a_2})\bigr){{< /m >}}.

The pole radius {{< m >}}r < 1{{< /m >}} corresponds to the analog stability condition {{< m >}}\operatorname{Re}(s_{\text{pole}}) < 0{{< /m >}} from {{< episode-ref ep="28" >}}EP28{{< /episode-ref >}}; the bilinear transform maps left half-plane to interior of unit disk.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Bilinear Transform" label="Definition 37.3" >}}
The **bilinear transform** is the substitution

{{< dm >}}s \;\longleftarrow\; \frac{2}{T} \cdot \frac{1 - z^{-1}}{1 + z^{-1}}{{< /dm >}}

where {{< m >}}T = 1/f_s{{< /m >}} is the sampling period and {{< m >}}f_s{{< /m >}} is the sample rate. Solving for {{< m >}}z{{< /m >}} in terms of {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}z = \frac{1 + sT/2}{1 - sT/2}{{< /dm >}}

This is a **Mobius transformation** mapping the {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}-plane to the {{< m >}}z{{< /m >}}-plane. It maps the imaginary axis ({{< m >}}s = i\Omega{{< /m >}}) to the unit circle ({{< m >}}|z|=1{{< /m >}}) and the open left half-plane ({{< m >}}\operatorname{Re}(s) < 0{{< /m >}}) to the open unit disk ({{< m >}}|z| < 1{{< /m >}}).
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Frequency Warping" label="Definition 37.4" >}}
Under the bilinear transform, the digital frequency {{< m >}}\omega \in [0, \pi]{{< /m >}} (in radians per sample) and the analog frequency {{< m >}}\Omega{{< /m >}} (in radians per second) are related by the **warping equation**:

{{< dm >}}\Omega = \frac{2}{T}\tan\!\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right){{< /dm >}}

or equivalently {{< m >}}\omega = 2\arctan(\Omega T/2){{< /m >}}. This is a bijection from {{< m >}}[0,\pi]{{< /m >}} to {{< m >}}[0, \infty){{< /m >}}, but it is **non-linear**: equal intervals in {{< m >}}\omega{{< /m >}} do not correspond to equal intervals in {{< m >}}\Omega{{< /m >}}.

To design a digital filter with a specified characteristic frequency {{< m >}}f_0{{< /m >}} (in Hz), one must **pre-warp** the target to the analog prototype frequency

{{< dm >}}\Omega_0 = \frac{2}{T}\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi f_0}{f_s}\right){{< /dm >}}

and then design the analog prototype at {{< m >}}\Omega_0{{< /m >}} before applying the bilinear substitution.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Quality Factor Q and Pole Radius" label="Definition 37.5" >}}
For a conjugate biquad pole pair at {{< m >}}z_{1,2} = r\,e^{\pm i\theta}{{< /m >}}, the **pole angle** {{< m >}}\theta \approx 2\pi f_0 / f_s{{< /m >}} encodes the center frequency and the **pole radius** {{< m >}}r{{< /m >}} encodes the bandwidth. The quality factor is approximately

{{< dm >}}Q \approx \frac{\sin\theta}{2(1-r)}{{< /dm >}}

for {{< m >}}r{{< /m >}} close to 1. As {{< m >}}Q \to \infty{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}r \to 1{{< /m >}}: the poles approach the unit circle and the peak height grows without bound. At {{< m >}}r = 1{{< /m >}} (poles on the unit circle) the filter is **marginally unstable** (undamped oscillator).
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Envelope Follower (Compressor)" label="Definition 37.6" >}}
A **first-order IIR envelope follower** estimates the instantaneous amplitude of a signal {{< m >}}x[n]{{< /m >}} by the recursion

{{< dm >}}e[n] = \alpha\,e[n-1] + (1-\alpha)\,|x[n]|{{< /dm >}}

where the **smoothing coefficient** is

{{< dm >}}\alpha = e^{-1/(f_s \cdot \tau)}{{< /dm >}}

and {{< m >}}\tau > 0{{< /m >}} is the **time constant** in seconds. For attack and release, separate coefficients {{< m >}}\alpha_A{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}\alpha_R{{< /m >}} (with {{< m >}}\tau_A \ll \tau_R{{< /m >}}) are applied depending on whether the signal is rising or falling.

The gain reduction applied by a compressor with threshold {{< m >}}T_{\text{dB}}{{< /m >}} and ratio {{< m >}}R:1{{< /m >}} is (in dB):

{{< dm >}}G_{\text{red}}[n] = \frac{1 - 1/R}{1}\,\max\!\bigl(e_{\text{dB}}[n] - T_{\text{dB}},\;0\bigr){{< /dm >}}

The compressor is therefore a **time-varying gain** {{< m >}}g[n] = 10^{-G_{\text{red}}[n]/20}{{< /m >}} multiplied sample-by-sample.
{{< /definition >}}

## Main Theorems

{{< theorem name="Biquad Transfer Function and Stability" label="Theorem 37.1" >}}
Let {{< m >}}H(z){{< /m >}} be a biquad with real coefficients as in Definition 37.1. Then:

1. {{< m >}}H(z){{< /m >}} is BIBO stable if and only if both roots of {{< m >}}p(z) = z^2 + a_1 z + a_2{{< /m >}} satisfy {{< m >}}|z| < 1{{< /m >}}.

2. The **Schur-Cohn stability conditions** for {{< m >}}p(z){{< /m >}} are equivalent to:
{{< dm >}}|a_2| < 1, \quad |a_1| < 1 + a_2{{< /dm >}}

3. The **frequency response** of a stable biquad is obtained by evaluating {{< m >}}H{{< /m >}} on the unit circle:
{{< dm >}}H(e^{i\omega}) = \frac{b_0 e^{2i\omega} + b_1 e^{i\omega} + b_2}{e^{2i\omega} + a_1 e^{i\omega} + a_2}{{< /dm >}}

and its magnitude is determined entirely by the distances from {{< m >}}e^{i\omega}{{< /m >}} to the poles and zeros in the {{< m >}}z{{< /m >}}-plane.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
**(Part 1.)** The output {{< m >}}Y(z) = H(z) X(z){{< /m >}}. For an bounded input, the output is bounded if and only if the partial-fraction expansion of {{< m >}}H(z){{< /m >}} has only terms {{< m >}}A/(z - z_k){{< /m >}} with {{< m >}}|z_k| < 1{{< /m >}}, so that the corresponding time-domain sequences {{< m >}}A z_k^n u[n]{{< /m >}} decay. Poles with {{< m >}}|z_k| \geq 1{{< /m >}} produce non-decaying or growing terms, violating BIBO stability.

**(Part 2.)** Write {{< m >}}p(z) = z^2 + a_1 z + a_2{{< /m >}}. The Jury stability test (equivalently, Schur-Cohn criterion) for a degree-2 polynomial states: both roots inside the unit disk if and only if
{{< dm >}}p(1) > 0, \quad p(-1) > 0, \quad |a_2| < 1{{< /dm >}}

Computing: {{< m >}}p(1) = 1 + a_1 + a_2 > 0{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}p(-1) = 1 - a_1 + a_2 > 0{{< /m >}}. These two inequalities together say {{< m >}}|a_1| < 1 + a_2{{< /m >}}, and together with {{< m >}}|a_2| < 1{{< /m >}} they form the stated conditions.

**(Part 3.)** The unit circle is the set {{< m >}}z = e^{i\omega}{{< /m >}} for {{< m >}}\omega \in [0, 2\pi){{< /m >}}. Substituting into {{< m >}}H(z){{< /m >}} gives the frequency response directly. Writing the numerator as {{< m >}}b_0(z - z_1)(z-z_2){{< /m >}} and denominator as {{< m >}}(z - p_1)(z-p_2){{< /m >}}, the magnitude is

{{< dm >}}|H(e^{i\omega})| = |b_0| \cdot \frac{|e^{i\omega} - z_1|\,|e^{i\omega} - z_2|}{|e^{i\omega} - p_1|\,|e^{i\omega} - p_2|}{{< /dm >}}

Each factor is the Euclidean distance from the evaluation point {{< m >}}e^{i\omega}{{< /m >}} on the unit circle to the corresponding pole or zero in {{< m >}}\mathbb{C}{{< /m >}}. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Bilinear Transform: Stability Preservation" label="Theorem 37.2" >}}
Let {{< m >}}H_a(s){{< /m >}} be a stable analog filter with all poles in the open left half-plane: {{< m >}}\operatorname{Re}(s_k) < 0{{< /m >}} for all poles {{< m >}}s_k{{< /m >}}. Define the digital filter obtained by the bilinear substitution of Definition 37.3:

{{< dm >}}H(z) = H_a\!\left(\frac{2}{T}\cdot\frac{1-z^{-1}}{1+z^{-1}}\right){{< /dm >}}

Then {{< m >}}H(z){{< /m >}} is BIBO stable: all its poles lie strictly inside the unit circle.

Furthermore, the bilinear transform is **bijective**: the imaginary axis {{< m >}}\{s : \operatorname{Re}(s)=0\}{{< /m >}} maps exactly onto the unit circle {{< m >}}\{z : |z|=1\}{{< /m >}}, and no aliasing of poles occurs (unlike the impulse-invariance method).
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
Write {{< m >}}s = \sigma + i\Omega{{< /m >}}. Under the bilinear map {{< m >}}z = (1 + sT/2)/(1 - sT/2){{< /m >}}, compute:

{{< dm >}}|z|^2 = \frac{|1 + sT/2|^2}{|1 - sT/2|^2} = \frac{(1 + \sigma T/2)^2 + (\Omega T/2)^2}{(1 - \sigma T/2)^2 + (\Omega T/2)^2}{{< /dm >}}

If {{< m >}}\sigma < 0{{< /m >}} (left half-plane), then {{< m >}}(1 + \sigma T/2) < (1 - \sigma T/2){{< /m >}}, so the numerator {{< m >}} < {{< /m >}} denominator, giving {{< m >}}|z| < 1{{< /m >}}. If {{< m >}}\sigma = 0{{< /m >}} then numerator = denominator so {{< m >}}|z| = 1{{< /m >}}. If {{< m >}}\sigma > 0{{< /m >}} then {{< m >}}|z| > 1{{< /m >}}.

Thus the bilinear map is an order-preserving bijection between the three regions {{< m >}}\{\operatorname{Re}(s) \lessgtr 0\}{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}\{|z| \lessgtr 1\}{{< /m >}}. Since each analog pole at {{< m >}}\operatorname{Re}(s_k) < 0{{< /m >}} maps to a digital pole with {{< m >}}|z_k| < 1{{< /m >}}, stability is preserved.

Bijectivity follows from the fact that the Mobius transformation {{< m >}}z \mapsto (1+sT/2)/(1-sT/2){{< /m >}} is an invertible analytic map on {{< m >}}\hat{\mathbb{C}}{{< /m >}} (Riemann sphere); no two distinct {{< m >}}s{{< /m >}}-values map to the same {{< m >}}z{{< /m >}}-value. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Frequency Warping Correction" label="Theorem 37.3" >}}
Let {{< m >}}H_a(s){{< /m >}} be an analog prototype with a critical frequency at {{< m >}}\Omega_0{{< /m >}} (e.g., the {{< m >}}-3{{< /m >}} dB point of a lowpass, or the center of a bandpass). After the bilinear substitution with period {{< m >}}T{{< /m >}}, the digital filter {{< m >}}H(z){{< /m >}} has its critical feature at the **digital frequency**

{{< dm >}}\omega_0 = 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{\Omega_0 T}{2}\right){{< /dm >}}

To obtain a digital filter whose critical feature falls exactly at a desired digital frequency {{< m >}}\omega_d = 2\pi f_d / f_s{{< /m >}}, one must design the analog prototype at the **pre-warped analog frequency**:

{{< dm >}}\Omega_d = \frac{2}{T}\tan\!\left(\frac{\omega_d}{2}\right) = \frac{2}{T}\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi f_d}{f_s}\right){{< /dm >}}

The error in critical frequency if warping correction is omitted grows as {{< m >}}f_d / f_s{{< /m >}} increases: for {{< m >}}f_d = 0.1 f_s{{< /m >}} the error is under 1%, but for {{< m >}}f_d = 0.4 f_s{{< /m >}} it exceeds 25%.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
On the unit circle {{< m >}}z = e^{i\omega}{{< /m >}}, the bilinear substitution becomes:

{{< dm >}}s = \frac{2}{T}\cdot\frac{1 - e^{-i\omega}}{1 + e^{-i\omega}}{{< /dm >}}

Multiply numerator and denominator by {{< m >}}e^{i\omega/2}{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}s = \frac{2}{T}\cdot\frac{e^{i\omega/2} - e^{-i\omega/2}}{e^{i\omega/2} + e^{-i\omega/2}} = \frac{2}{T}\cdot\frac{2i\sin(\omega/2)}{2\cos(\omega/2)} = \frac{2i}{T}\tan\!\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right){{< /dm >}}

So the purely imaginary value {{< m >}}s = i\Omega{{< /m >}} corresponds to {{< m >}}\Omega = (2/T)\tan(\omega/2){{< /m >}}, i.e. {{< m >}}\omega = 2\arctan(\Omega T/2){{< /m >}}. This is a bijective but non-linear correspondence.

Suppose the analog prototype has a feature (zero, pole, or gain crossing) at {{< m >}}\Omega_0{{< /m >}}. After bilinear substitution, that feature migrates to the digital frequency {{< m >}}\omega_0 = 2\arctan(\Omega_0 T/2){{< /m >}}, not the intended {{< m >}}\omega_d{{< /m >}} (unless {{< m >}}\Omega_0 = (2/T)\tan(\omega_d/2){{< /m >}} was chosen by pre-warping). Solving for {{< m >}}\Omega_0 = \Omega_d{{< /m >}} recovers the stated pre-warp formula. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Magnitude Response from Pole-Zero Distances" label="Theorem 37.4" >}}
Let {{< m >}}H(z){{< /m >}} have poles {{< m >}}p_1, p_2{{< /m >}} and zeros {{< m >}}z_1, z_2{{< /m >}} (all complex, possibly conjugate pairs). At angular frequency {{< m >}}\omega{{< /m >}}, let

{{< dm >}}d_{z_k}(\omega) = |e^{i\omega} - z_k|, \quad d_{p_k}(\omega) = |e^{i\omega} - p_k|{{< /dm >}}

denote the Euclidean distance from the unit-circle point {{< m >}}e^{i\omega}{{< /m >}} to zero {{< m >}}z_k{{< /m >}} and pole {{< m >}}p_k{{< /m >}}, respectively. Then

{{< dm >}}|H(e^{i\omega})| = |b_0| \cdot \frac{d_{z_1}(\omega)\,d_{z_2}(\omega)}{d_{p_1}(\omega)\,d_{p_2}(\omega)}{{< /dm >}}

**Corollary.** As a pole {{< m >}}p_k{{< /m >}} approaches the unit circle ({{< m >}}|p_k| \to 1{{< /m >}}) from inside, the distance {{< m >}}d_{p_k}(\omega_k) \to 0{{< /m >}} at the angle {{< m >}}\omega_k = \arg(p_k){{< /m >}}, and therefore {{< m >}}|H(e^{i\omega_k})| \to \infty{{< /m >}}: the gain peak height is inversely proportional to the distance from the nearest pole to the unit circle.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
Factor the numerator and denominator of {{< m >}}H(z) = b_0(z-z_1)(z-z_2)/[(z-p_1)(z-p_2)]{{< /m >}}. Evaluate at {{< m >}}z = e^{i\omega}{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}H(e^{i\omega}) = b_0 \cdot \frac{(e^{i\omega} - z_1)(e^{i\omega}-z_2)}{(e^{i\omega}-p_1)(e^{i\omega}-p_2)}{{< /dm >}}

Taking absolute values and using {{< m >}}|AB| = |A||B|{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}|A/B| = |A|/|B|{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}|H(e^{i\omega})| = |b_0|\cdot\frac{|e^{i\omega}-z_1|\,|e^{i\omega}-z_2|}{|e^{i\omega}-p_1|\,|e^{i\omega}-p_2|}{{< /dm >}}

Each factor {{< m >}}|e^{i\omega} - c|{{< /m >}} is precisely the Euclidean distance {{< m >}}d_c(\omega){{< /m >}} between two points in {{< m >}}\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}^2{{< /m >}}. This is the stated formula.

For the corollary: if {{< m >}}p_1 = r\,e^{i\theta}{{< /m >}} with {{< m >}}r \to 1{{< /m >}}, then at {{< m >}}\omega = \theta{{< /m >}},

{{< dm >}}d_{p_1}(\theta) = |e^{i\theta} - r\,e^{i\theta}| = |1 - r| \to 0{{< /dm >}}

The denominator vanishes, so {{< m >}}|H(e^{i\theta})| \to \infty{{< /m >}}. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Serial Biquad Product Theorem" label="Theorem 37.5" >}}
Let {{< m >}}H_1(z), \ldots, H_N(z){{< /m >}} be biquads in a **serial cascade** (output of each feeds input of the next). Then:

1. The combined transfer function is the product:
{{< dm >}}H_{\text{total}}(z) = \prod_{k=1}^{N} H_k(z){{< /dm >}}

2. The combined system is BIBO stable if and only if every individual biquad is stable (no pole of any {{< m >}}H_k{{< /m >}} lies on or outside the unit circle).

3. The combined pole-zero set is the **union** of the individual pole and zero sets (counted with multiplicity), and the combined magnitude response is the **product** of the individual magnitude responses:
{{< dm >}}|H_{\text{total}}(e^{i\omega})| = \prod_{k=1}^{N} |H_k(e^{i\omega})|{{< /dm >}}

In decibels: {{< m >}}20\log_{10}|H_{\text{total}}| = \sum_k 20\log_{10}|H_k|{{< /dm >}}.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
**(Part 1.)** In the cascade, the output of stage {{< m >}}k{{< /m >}} is {{< m >}}Y_k(z) = H_k(z) Y_{k-1}(z){{< /m >}}, with {{< m >}}Y_0(z) = X(z){{< /m >}}. By induction, {{< m >}}Y_N(z) = \bigl(\prod_k H_k(z)\bigr) X(z){{< /m >}}.

**(Part 2.)** The poles of {{< m >}}H_{\text{total}}{{< /m >}} are the union of the poles of each factor (assuming no pole-zero cancellation). A product of rational functions has {{< m >}}|z_k| < 1{{< /m >}} for all poles if and only if each factor satisfies this. Pole-zero cancellations that would place a cancelled pole exactly on the unit circle are excluded by the assumption that each {{< m >}}H_k{{< /m >}} is individually stable.

**(Part 3.)** Taking absolute values of the product: {{< m >}}|H_{\text{total}}(e^{i\omega})| = \prod_k |H_k(e^{i\omega})|{{< /m >}}, since absolute value is multiplicative. Converting to dB uses the identity {{< m >}}\log|AB| = \log|A| + \log|B|{{< /m >}}. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

## Numerical Examples

**Example 1: Peaking EQ at 1 kHz, {{< m >}}Q=2{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}G=+6{{< /m >}} dB at {{< m >}}f_s=44100{{< /m >}} Hz.**

From the Bristow-Johnson Cookbook (peaking EQ section), set {{< m >}}A = 10^{G/40} = 10^{6/40} \approx 1.4125{{< /m >}} and

{{< dm >}}\omega_0 = 2\pi f_0 / f_s = 2\pi \cdot 1000 / 44100 \approx 0.14247 \text{ rad/sample}{{< /dm >}}

{{< dm >}}\alpha = \sin(\omega_0)/(2Q) = \sin(0.14247)/2 \approx 0.14197/2 \approx 0.03557{{< /dm >}}

The five coefficients are:

{{< dm >}}b_0 = 1 + \alpha A \approx 1.05025, \quad b_1 = -2\cos\omega_0 \approx -1.97984, \quad b_2 = 1 - \alpha A \approx 0.94975{{< /dm >}}
{{< dm >}}a_0 = 1 + \alpha/A \approx 1.02514, \quad a_1 = -2\cos\omega_0 \approx -1.97984, \quad a_2 = 1 - \alpha/A \approx 0.97486{{< /dm >}}

(Dividing through by {{< m >}}a_0{{< /m >}} normalises to the standard form.) The pole radius is {{< m >}}r = \sqrt{a_2/a_0} \approx \sqrt{0.95092} \approx 0.9752{{< /m >}}, well inside the unit circle. Gain at {{< m >}}f_0 = 1{{< /m >}} kHz: exactly {{< m >}}+6{{< /m >}} dB by construction.

**Example 2: Effect of increasing Q from 1 to 8.**

Keep {{< m >}}f_0 = 1{{< /m >}} kHz, {{< m >}}G = +6{{< /m >}} dB, {{< m >}}f_s = 44100{{< /m >}} Hz. The parameter {{< m >}}\alpha = \sin\omega_0 / (2Q){{< /m >}} controls the bandwidth:

| {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} | {{< m >}}\alpha{{< /m >}} | Pole radius {{< m >}}r{{< /m >}} | Distance to unit circle {{< m >}}1-r{{< /m >}} | Approx. bandwidth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.0712 | 0.9294 | 0.0706 | ~1000 Hz |
| 2 | 0.0356 | 0.9650 | 0.0350 | ~500 Hz |
| 4 | 0.0178 | 0.9824 | 0.0176 | ~250 Hz |
| 8 | 0.0089 | 0.9912 | 0.0088 | ~125 Hz |

Doubling {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} approximately halves the pole-to-unit-circle distance, halves the bandwidth, and narrows the EQ peak. As the table shows, at {{< m >}}Q = 8{{< /m >}} the poles are only {{< m >}}0.88\%{{< /m >}} of the unit-circle radius away from instability.

**Example 3: Compressor envelope follower time constants.**

A typical drum bus compressor uses {{< m >}}\tau_A = 5{{< /m >}} ms (attack) and {{< m >}}\tau_R = 100{{< /m >}} ms (release) at {{< m >}}f_s = 44100{{< /m >}} Hz:

{{< dm >}}\alpha_A = e^{-1/(44100 \times 0.005)} = e^{-1/220.5} \approx e^{-0.004535} \approx 0.99548{{< /dm >}}
{{< dm >}}\alpha_R = e^{-1/(44100 \times 0.100)} = e^{-1/4410} \approx e^{-0.000227} \approx 0.99977{{< /dm >}}

The attack filter has a pole at {{< m >}}z = \alpha_A \approx 0.99548{{< /m >}} and the release filter has a pole at {{< m >}}z = \alpha_R \approx 0.99977{{< /m >}} — both on the positive real axis just inside the unit circle. The closer the pole to {{< m >}}z=1{{< /m >}}, the more sluggish (longer time constant) the envelope follower.

**Example 4: Signal chain pole count.**

A typical vocal recording path for {{< episode-ref ep="28" >}}EP28{{< /episode-ref >}} contains:
- Microphone diaphragm (1 biquad, EP28): 2 poles, 2 zeros
- High-pass filter (1 biquad): 2 poles, 2 zeros
- 5-band parametric EQ (5 biquads): 10 poles, 10 zeros
- Compressor envelope follower (1 first-order IIR): 1 pole

Total: **15 poles, 14 zeros** shaping the final audio. The constellation of these 15 points in the {{< m >}}z{{< /m >}}-plane, by Theorem 37.4 and 37.5, is the entire mathematical content of "what this recording sounds like."

## Musical Connection

{{< musical-connection >}}
**Every EQ gesture is a pole migration.**

When a mastering engineer draws a narrow boost at 3 kHz on a parametric EQ, the intuitive description is "adding presence." The mathematical description is: two complex-conjugate poles move outward by a fraction of a percent, closer to the unit circle at angle {{< m >}}\theta = 2\pi \times 3000/f_s{{< /m >}}. Theorem 37.4 says the gain at that frequency is inversely proportional to the remaining distance to the unit circle — so the boost height is literally the reciprocal of a length in the complex plane.

The Q knob controls pole orbit radius. Musicians describe high-Q boosts as "nasal" or "ringing" — because a pole very near the unit circle has a long impulse response tail (the filter rings at its own natural frequency, just like the underdamped microphone diaphragm from {{< episode-ref ep="28" >}}EP28{{< /episode-ref >}}). Low-Q settings produce broad shelving shapes, corresponding to poles comfortably inside the circle with short impulse responses.

Dynamic range compression adds a time dimension: the gain {{< m >}}g[n]{{< /m >}} modulates slowly at the envelope follower's time constant {{< m >}}\tau{{< /m >}}. Fast attack (small {{< m >}}\tau_A{{< /m >}}) captures transients immediately, reducing the "punch" of drums; slow attack lets transients through, preserving their spike in the time-domain waveform. The trade-off is fundamental and cannot be evaded: it is the discrete-time version of the time-frequency uncertainty principle — a filter with a short impulse response (fast response to level changes) must have a broad frequency response (less selective). The envelope follower's pole at {{< m >}}z = \alpha{{< /m >}} close to 1 has a 3 dB bandwidth of only {{< m >}}f_s(1-\alpha)/(2\pi){{< /m >}} Hz — extremely narrow — which is why the gain changes feel smooth, not jittery.

The signal chain of Theorem 37.5 makes explicit what every recording engineer knows intuitively: the sounds of different studios, different mic chains, and different mastering processors are distinguishable because their pole-zero constellations differ. "Analog warmth" (saturation harmonics aside) is a particular distribution of poles near the real axis at low frequencies. "Air" is poles near {{< m >}}\theta = \pi/3{{< /m >}} with small {{< m >}}1-r{{< /m >}}. The vocabulary of timbral description is a folk taxonomy of pole-zero constellations.

{{< episode-ref ep="39" >}}EP39{{< /episode-ref >}} extends this further: a reverb is a feedback delay network whose transfer function is a high-order rational function with many poles distributed across the unit disk — in the limit, approaching a dense cloud that produces the perception of diffuse space.
{{< /musical-connection >}}

## Limits and Open Questions

1. **Fixed-point coefficient quantisation.** On embedded hardware (guitar pedals, hardware EQ units) the biquad coefficients are stored as fixed-point integers. For high-Q filters near the unit circle, tiny rounding errors in {{< m >}}a_1, a_2{{< /m >}} can displace poles outside the unit circle, causing instability. The standard remedy is to use second-order sections (Direct Form II Transposed) and to limit {{< m >}}Q{{< /m >}} based on word length, but no universal rule covers all parameter ranges. This is an active concern in embedded DSP design.

2. **Non-minimum phase zeros.** The Bristow-Johnson peaking EQ formulas place zeros symmetrically with respect to the unit circle (if a zero is at radius {{< m >}}r{{< /m >}}, its reflection at {{< m >}}1/r{{< /m >}} is also a zero). Minimum-phase designs place all zeros inside the unit circle, giving the fastest impulse response; but some filter types (linear-phase FIR, all-pass sections) deliberately use zeros outside the unit disk. The interaction between minimum-phase and non-minimum-phase sections in a serial chain is not always intuitive.

3. **Compressor parameter interactions.** The standard compressor model treats attack and release as independent first-order IIR filters. In practice, the knee shape, look-ahead (delay compensation), and program-dependent release create higher-order dynamics that a single-pole envelope follower does not capture. A rigorous mathematical model of compressor transient behaviour remains an open problem in audio engineering.

4. **Loudness and nonlinear perception.** The magnitude response {{< m >}}|H(e^{i\omega})|{{< /m >}} is a linear, time-invariant quantity. Human loudness perception is neither linear nor time-invariant: the equal-loudness contours (Fletcher-Munson curves) are level-dependent, and the auditory system has its own compressive nonlinearity (outer hair cell electromotility). Matching a linear biquad EQ to a perceptual loudness target requires additional psychoacoustic modelling not captured by Theorem 37.4.

5. **Multi-rate and oversampled biquads.** Many modern plug-ins run biquads at 2× or 4× oversampled rates to reduce frequency warping near Nyquist and to allow the nonlinear saturation components (operating on the upsampled signal) to alias-free back down. The interplay between the bilinear transform pre-warping (Theorem 37.3) and the oversampling ratio introduces design dependencies that are not yet standardised in the literature.
