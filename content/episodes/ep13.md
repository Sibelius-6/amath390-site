## Definitions

{{< definition name="Polyrhythm" label="Definition 13.1" >}}
A **polyrhythm** {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}} is the simultaneous sounding of {{< m >}}m{{< /m >}} beats against {{< m >}}n{{< /m >}} beats within the same time span {{< m >}}T{{< /m >}}. The beat durations are {{< m >}}T/m{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}T/n{{< /m >}} respectively. The two sequences share the same start and end point but divide the interval differently.

Special cases:
- {{< m >}}2:3{{< /m >}} — **hemiola**: two against three; the most common polyrhythm in Western art music.
- {{< m >}}3:4{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}4:5{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}3:2:4:5:6{{< /m >}} — increasingly complex combinations.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Rhythmic LCM Grid" label="Definition 13.2" >}}
Given a polyrhythm {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}}, the **common grid** is the finest uniform subdivision that contains all beat onsets of both rhythmic streams. The number of grid cells per span {{< m >}}T{{< /m >}} is {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m, n){{< /m >}}.

More generally, for a {{< m >}}k{{< /m >}}-layer polyrhythm with layers {{< m >}}m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_k{{< /m >}}, the common grid has {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m_1, \ldots, m_k){{< /m >}} subdivisions.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Rhythm-Pitch Threshold" label="Definition 13.3" >}}
The **rhythm-pitch threshold** is the critical repetition rate, approximately 20 Hz (individual variation: 15–30 Hz), below which the auditory system perceives a periodic pulse as a **rhythm** (individual events are counted), and above which it perceives the pulse as a **pitch** (a continuous tone at that frequency).

This threshold is a property of auditory processing, not of the signal itself. The mathematical structure — a periodic sequence with period {{< m >}}1/f{{< /m >}} — is identical on both sides of the boundary.
{{< /definition >}}

{{< definition name="Spectral Consonance (Helmholtz)" label="Definition 13.4" >}}
Given two periodic signals with frequencies {{< m >}}f_1{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}f_2{{< /m >}} in ratio {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}} (lowest terms), their **combined waveform** repeats with period {{< m >}}T = \text{lcm}(m, n) / f_1{{< /m >}}. The **Helmholtz consonance** of the interval is defined as the repetition frequency of the combined waveform:

{{< dm >}}C = \frac{f_1}{\text{lcm}(m, n)} = \frac{1}{T}{{< /dm >}}

Higher {{< m >}}C{{< /m >}} (i.e., smaller {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m, n){{< /m >}}, i.e., simpler integer ratio) corresponds to greater consonance.
{{< /definition >}}

## Main Theorems

{{< theorem name="LCM Periodicity Theorem" label="Theorem 13.1" >}}
In a polyrhythm {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}}, the two rhythmic streams **realign** (both streams have a beat onset simultaneously) for the first time after exactly {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m, n){{< /m >}} minimal grid subdivisions, and then periodically every {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m, n){{< /m >}} subdivisions thereafter.

For Jacob Collier's five-layer polyrhythm {{< m >}}2:3:4:5:6{{< /m >}}:

{{< dm >}}\text{lcm}(2, 3, 4, 5, 6) = 60{{< /dm >}}

A complete cycle requires 60 minimal subdivisions.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
Let the minimal subdivision have duration {{< m >}}\delta = T/\text{lcm}(m,n){{< /m >}}. Stream {{< m >}}m{{< /m >}} places beats at positions {{< m >}}0, \text{lcm}(m,n)/m, 2\cdot\text{lcm}(m,n)/m, \ldots{{< /m >}}, and stream {{< m >}}n{{< /m >}} at {{< m >}}0, \text{lcm}(m,n)/n, 2\cdot\text{lcm}(m,n)/n, \ldots{{< /m >}} — all integers, since {{< m >}}m \mid \text{lcm}(m,n){{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}n \mid \text{lcm}(m,n){{< /m >}}.

The first positive position where both streams align is the smallest positive integer {{< m >}}k{{< /m >}} with {{< m >}}m \mid k{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}n \mid k{{< /m >}}, which by definition is {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m, n){{< /m >}}. All subsequent alignments occur at integer multiples of {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m,n){{< /m >}}.

For the five-layer case: {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(2,3) = 6{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(6,4) = 12{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(12,5) = 60{{< /m >}}, {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(60,6) = 60{{< /m >}}. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="GCD Irreducibility Theorem" label="Theorem 13.2" >}}
A polyrhythm {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}} is **irreducible** (cannot be simplified to a coarser polyrhythm) if and only if {{< m >}}\gcd(m, n) = 1{{< /m >}}.

If {{< m >}}\gcd(m, n) = d > 1{{< /m >}}, then {{< m >}}m:n = (m/d):(n/d){{< /m >}}, a simpler polyrhythm with the same structure repeated {{< m >}}d{{< /m >}} times.

Furthermore, the **complexity** of the polyrhythm — measured by {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m, n) / \min(m, n){{< /m >}} — is maximized when {{< m >}}\gcd(m, n) = 1{{< /m >}}.
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
Write {{< m >}}m = d \cdot m'{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}n = d \cdot n'{{< /m >}} with {{< m >}}\gcd(m', n') = 1{{< /m >}}. Then {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m, n) = d \cdot \text{lcm}(m', n') = d \cdot m' n'{{< /m >}}. The polyrhythm {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}} consists of {{< m >}}d{{< /m >}} complete repetitions of the irreducible polyrhythm {{< m >}}m':n'{{< /m >}}, each of duration {{< m >}}T/d{{< /m >}}.

When {{< m >}}\gcd(m, n) = 1{{< /m >}}: {{< m >}}\text{lcm}(m, n) = mn{{< /m >}}, the maximum possible value. No simplification exists.

Connection to EP12: there, {{< m >}}\gcd(7, 12) = 1{{< /m >}} ensured that adding 7 semitones repeatedly visited all 12 pitch classes before returning to the start — the generator of {{< m >}}\mathbb{Z}_{12}{{< /m >}}. Here, {{< m >}}\gcd(m, n) = 1{{< /m >}} ensures the polyrhythm visits all {{< m >}}mn{{< /m >}} grid positions before repeating. Same theorem, different domain. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< theorem name="Rhythm-Pitch Duality" label="Theorem 13.3" >}}
Let {{< m >}}R_{m,n}(f){{< /m >}} denote the rhythmic sequence produced by two streams at repetition rates {{< m >}}m \cdot f{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}n \cdot f{{< /m >}} Hz. As {{< m >}}f{{< /m >}} increases:

- For {{< m >}}f \ll 20 \text{ Hz}{{< /m >}}: the auditory system perceives two independent rhythmic streams with ratio {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}}.
- For {{< m >}}f \gg 20 \text{ Hz}{{< /m >}}: the auditory system perceives two simultaneous pitches at frequencies {{< m >}}m \cdot f{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}n \cdot f{{< /m >}} Hz, forming the musical interval with frequency ratio {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}}.

The mathematical structure is invariant under this perceptual transition: the ratio {{< m >}}m:n{{< /m >}} is preserved. The transition is a property of auditory processing, not of the acoustic signal.

**Corollary**: The polyrhythm {{< m >}}2:3{{< /m >}} at {{< m >}}f = 100\,\text{Hz}{{< /m >}} produces pitches at 200 Hz and 300 Hz — a perfect fifth ({{< m >}}3:2{{< /m >}} frequency ratio, approximately 702 cents).
{{< /theorem >}}

{{< proof >}}
The two pulse trains have frequencies {{< m >}}m \cdot f{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}n \cdot f{{< /m >}}. Their Fourier spectra have energy concentrated at integer multiples of {{< m >}}m \cdot f{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}n \cdot f{{< /m >}} respectively.

When {{< m >}}m \cdot f < 20{{< /m >}} Hz: the inter-onset interval {{< m >}}1/(m \cdot f) > 50{{< /m >}} ms. The auditory system's temporal resolution (~1–5 ms) can track individual onsets, and the brain counts discrete events as rhythm.

When {{< m >}}m \cdot f > 20{{< /m >}} Hz: the inter-onset interval {{< m >}}1/(m \cdot f) < 50{{< /m >}} ms. The auditory system integrates the pulse train as a continuous periodic signal (pitch fusion). The perceived pitch corresponds to the repetition frequency {{< m >}}m \cdot f{{< /m >}}.

The frequency ratio {{< m >}}m \cdot f : n \cdot f = m:n{{< /m >}} is identical in both regimes. The perceptual change is a consequence of the auditory system's temporal integration window, not a mathematical discontinuity. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

{{< proposition label="Prop 13.1" name="Harmonic Series from Integer Ratios" >}}
Let {{< m >}}f_0 > 0{{< /m >}} be a fundamental frequency. The integer multiples {{< m >}}2f_0, 3f_0, 4f_0, 5f_0, 6f_0{{< /m >}} (the second through sixth harmonics) form the frequency ratios {{< m >}}2:3:4:5:6{{< /m >}}.

These ratios correspond to:
- {{< m >}}4:5{{< /m >}} — major third (386 cents just intonation)
- {{< m >}}4:6 = 2:3{{< /m >}} — perfect fifth (702 cents)
- {{< m >}}4:5:6{{< /m >}} — just major triad
- {{< m >}}2:3:4:5:6{{< /m >}} — the same integer sequence that defines Jacob Collier's five-layer polyrhythm.

Consequently, accelerating a {{< m >}}2:3:4:5:6{{< /m >}} polyrhythm past 20 Hz yields a just major chord above {{< m >}}2f_0{{< /m >}}.
{{< /proposition >}}

{{< proof >}}
Direct calculation. The ratios within {{< m >}}\{2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}{{< /m >}}: {{< m >}}5/4 = 1.25{{< /m >}} (major third, just value), {{< m >}}6/4 = 3/2 = 1.5{{< /m >}} (perfect fifth, just value), {{< m >}}6/5 = 1.2{{< /m >}} (minor third, just value). The triad {{< m >}}\{4, 5, 6\}{{< /m >}} has all three of these — a just major triad. The Helmholtz consonance of the combined waveform of {{< m >}}\{2f_0, 3f_0, 4f_0, 5f_0, 6f_0\}{{< /m >}} is {{< m >}}f_0 / \text{lcm}(2,3,4,5,6) = f_0/60{{< /m >}}. {{< m >}}\square{{< /m >}}
{{< /proof >}}

## The Collier Limit: 30:31

Jacob Collier performs a {{< m >}}30:31{{< /m >}} polyrhythm. This is an extreme edge case:

{{< dm >}}\text{lcm}(30, 31) = 930 \quad (\text{since } \gcd(30, 31) = 1){{< /dm >}}

A full cycle requires 930 minimal subdivisions — near the limit of human temporal tracking. The perceptual effect resembles irrational ratios without actually being irrational.

Compare to the result from EP11: {{< m >}}\log_2 3{{< /m >}} is irrational, so the sequence {{< m >}}\{2^m : m \in \mathbb{Z}\} \cap \{3^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\} = \{1\}{{< /m >}} — powers of 2 and powers of 3 never coincide (except at 1). A true {{< m >}}\sqrt{2}:1{{< /m >}} polyrhythm would **never** realign.

The ratio {{< m >}}30:31{{< /m >}} is rational (realigns after 930 units) but approximates the perceptual experience of irrationality. This is an aesthetic strategy: maintain mathematical controllability while approaching the boundary of perceptual chaos.

## Limits and Open Questions

1. **The 20 Hz threshold is not sharp.** Individual variation spans approximately 15–30 Hz. The "boundary" is a statistical description of population-level auditory processing, not a physical constant. Some listeners with absolute pitch or musical training may perceive rhythm-to-pitch transitions earlier.

2. **Euclidean rhythms and the Toussaint conjecture.** Godfried Toussaint conjectured (2005) that all "good" rhythms used in world music are Euclidean rhythms. While many examples are confirmed, a complete classification of "good rhythms" remains open.

3. **Rhythmic canons and the Fuglede conjecture.** Determining which subsets of {{< m >}}\mathbb{Z}_n{{< /m >}} can tile the group (rhythmic canons) is connected to spectral properties of the set via the discrete Fourier transform. Fuglede's conjecture (1974) proposed an equivalence between tiling and spectrality; it was disproved in {{< m >}}\mathbb{R}^d{{< /m >}} for {{< m >}}d \geq 3{{< /m >}} but remains open in {{< m >}}\mathbb{Z}{{< /m >}} and {{< m >}}\mathbb{R}^d{{< /m >}} for {{< m >}}d \leq 2{{< /m >}}.

4. **Cognitive limits of polyrhythm tracking.** Jacob Collier's {{< m >}}30:31{{< /m >}} has {{< m >}}\text{lcm} = 930{{< /m >}}. The cognitive limit for simultaneous rhythmic tracking is not well established theoretically. Empirical studies suggest 3–4 independent rhythmic streams as a working memory limit, but virtuosic performance (Collier, Nancarrow) routinely exceeds this — suggesting different neural mechanisms than explicit beat counting.

5. **Forward to EP26.** The power spectral density of rhythmic patterns and the {{< m >}}1/f{{< /m >}} fractal structure of musical rhythm (Hurst exponent analysis) generalize these ideas. A fractal rhythm is self-similar across time scales — the rhythm-pitch duality of this episode is the limiting case of that self-similarity as scale goes to zero.
